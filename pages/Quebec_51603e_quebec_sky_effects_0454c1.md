## Introduction

Most strange lights reported over [Quebec]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-quebecs-ufo/' | relative_url }}) can be explained without invoking an unusual vehicle. Bright planets can appear to hover or follow a car; meteors can cross half the province in seconds; aurora can form moving curtains, rays or glowing patches; and ice crystals can turn ordinary ground lighting into pillars, arcs or apparently suspended lights. Clouds can magnify the effect by hiding the source while displaying only its scattered glow.

These explanations do not prove that every Quebec UFO report has been solved. Some accounts lack the precise time, direction, weather data or [photographs]({{ 'photographs/' | relative_url }}) needed for a firm identification. The important distinction is between a genuinely extraordinary object and an observation that remains unidentified because the evidence is incomplete. Canada’s Sky Canada review stresses that “unidentified” does not imply extraterrestrial origin or freedom from natural explanation; better information may be enough to settle a case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.gc.ca">[Science.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFOs are by their very definition unidentified</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Management of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial...Until recently, the term “UFO” was used to describe aerial phenomena that could...</span></span></span>

## Astronomical objects commonly mistaken for craft

### Planets that seem to hover

Venus is one of the most persistent sources of UFO reports because it can be exceptionally bright in the western sky after sunset or in the east before sunrise. Against twilight or a dark, featureless horizon, it resembles an isolated lamp rather than a distant planet. Jupiter and the bright star Sirius can create similar impressions, especially when they are low in the sky and their light is distorted by turbulent air. NASA’s Night Sky Network specifically identifies Venus as a frequent source of reports and recommends comparing an observation with planet and star positions before treating it as unexplained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nightsky.jpl.nasa.gov/news/39/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nightsky.jpl.nasa.gov">[nightsky.jpl.nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nightsky.jpl.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Identifying UFOs and UAPs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Do you think you have spotted a UFO? A planet’s slow apparent movement can also be misleading. Someone driving through Quebec’s open countryside may see a bright object remain in roughly the same part of the windscreen for many kilometres. Because the planet is enormously farther away than trees, buildings and hills, the foreground changes while the light’s direction changes very little. The natural impression is that it is keeping pace with the vehicle.

An even stronger illusion can occur when a person stares at a lone light in darkness. Transport Canada describes **autokinesis**, in which a stationary light appears to shift or move when watched against a background with few visual references. Its [aviation]({{ 'aviation/' | relative_url }}) guidance notes that stars or other fixed lights can consequently be mistaken for moving objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/publications/system-safety-summer-briefing-kit-tp-14112/hazards-associated-flying-night-powerpoint-presentation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada Hazards Associated with Flying at Night</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Although we know that flowers and leaves are not really turning grey as the light diminishes, they do appear to be losing their colour be...</span></span></span>

These effects are especially relevant to reports containing phrases such as “hovered silently”, “moved when I moved”, “zigzagged slightly” or “stayed in one place for twenty minutes”. A long duration and lack of sound may seem impressive, but they actually fit a planet or bright star better than a nearby aircraft.

### Meteors and fireballs

Meteors produce a very different class of report: sudden, fast and often dramatic. A bright meteor, commonly called a fireball, may appear as a white, blue, green or orange object with a luminous tail. It can flare, fragment or vanish abruptly. Because the event occurs high in the atmosphere and may be seen across a wide region, witnesses in Montreal, the Eastern Townships, the Outaouais and neighbouring provinces can report the same light from apparently different directions.

Quebec has well-documented examples. On 21 September 2016, an all-sky camera in the University of Western Ontario meteor network recorded a fireball over Montreal that the Canadian Space Agency described as almost as bright as the full Moon. Instrumental confirmation transformed what might otherwise have entered local folklore as an unidentified object into a precisely timed astronomical event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/multimedia/search/video/18115" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asc-csa.gc.ca">[Canadian Space Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asc-csa.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canadian Space Agency Meteor over Montreal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canadian Space AgencyMeteor over Montreal - Canadian Space AgencySeptember 23, 2016 — 2016-09-23 - A meteor (fireball) almost as bright a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 23, 2016</span></span></span>

In November 2013, residents around Montreal and Ottawa reported a bright object accompanied in some places by a loud sound. Astronomers regarded the descriptions as consistent with a fireball, although the event escaped the available all-sky cameras. Fireballs also demonstrate why eyewitness estimates of altitude can be unreliable. A meteor tens of kilometres high may look as though it passed just above a road, treeline or aircraft. Sound, when it arrives, may be delayed because the light reaches the observer almost immediately while the pressure wave travels much more slowly. A flash followed by a boom is therefore not evidence that an object landed nearby.

### When several lights appear together

Astronomical conjunctions can create reports of formations. Venus and Jupiter sometimes appear close together, producing two brilliant lights that are brighter than the surrounding stars. Thin cloud can diffuse each point into a larger glow, while brief gaps in the cloud make the lights seem to brighten, fade or change position.

Groups of reports appearing on the same evening should therefore be compared with astronomical charts and meteor databases before being treated as a provincial “flap”. A surge in calls may represent many witnesses observing one conspicuous natural event rather than many separate objects.

## How clouds and ice reshape ordinary light

### Light pillars in Quebec winters

Quebec’s cold-season atmosphere is particularly capable of producing luminous structures. Flat ice crystals suspended in high cloud, ice fog or very light precipitation can reflect light towards an observer. The result may be a vertical column above the Sun, Moon, a streetlamp, a stadium or an industrial site. Environment and Climate Change Canada notes that ice crystals generate optical effects including halos, coronas and sun pillars.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.climat.weather.gc.ca/glossary_e.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: climat.weather.gc.ca">[climat.weather.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">climat.weather.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A type of fog composed of suspended particles of ice or ice crystals 20 to 100 microns resulting from the freezing of tiny supercooled wa...</span></span></span>

At night, several ground lights can each produce a pillar. Seen from a distance, the actual lamps may be hidden by buildings, woodland or the curve of the terrain, leaving only coloured bars apparently hanging in the sky. As the observer moves, different crystals line up with the light sources, so the display may seem to rearrange itself. Photographs often intensify the colours and make the columns appear more solid than they did to the eye.

This mechanism fits reports of:

The crystals do not need to form a dense cloud. A shallow layer of diamond dust or ice fog can be enough, particularly in northern and inland Quebec where severe winter conditions are common.

### Halos, arcs and false companions

The same family of ice-crystal effects can place bright spots beside the Sun or Moon. These may look like separate luminous objects or symmetrical “escort lights”. Circular halos can be partly hidden by cloud, leaving only short arcs or isolated patches. Without the rest of the ring, an observer may not recognise the geometry.

These displays are normally slow-moving because they depend on the position of the Sun or Moon. They can nevertheless change rapidly when cloud thickness varies. A bright patch may disappear, reappear farther along an arc or split into two visible sections, creating the impression of controlled movement.

### Searchlights and illumination on low cloud

Low cloud can act as a screen. Searchlights, event lighting, powerful commercial lamps or reflected urban illumination may produce bright patches with no visible beam connecting them to the ground. If several lights are being moved, their spots can circle, converge and separate above the cloud base.

This possibility is important when considering the famous [Hotel Bonaventure]({{ 'bonaventure/' | relative_url }}) sighting in Montreal on 7 November 1990. Witnesses watched a pattern of lights through or against cloud for several hours, and some inferred a vast dark craft connecting them. No proposed explanation has been conclusively demonstrated, so the case should not be labelled solved. Nevertheless, its central visual evidence consisted of lights interacting with a cloud layer, not a sharply resolved machine seen in clear air. That leaves cloud illumination, ice-crystal reflection or another optical process among the plausible classes of explanation.

The absence of an associated radar return does not rule out such effects. Radar would not be expected to detect a patch of illuminated cloud or a reflection created along the observer’s line of sight. Negative radar evidence can weigh against a large conventional aircraft in a particular position, but it cannot convert an optical display into proof of a solid object.

## Northern lights that do not look like photographs

Aurora is familiar in principle but not always recognised in practice. Popular images emphasise sweeping green curtains, whereas real displays may begin as a pale arc, a diffuse patch, upright rays or a colourless haze. Human night vision is relatively poor at detecting colour, so a weak aurora can look grey or white to the eye while a camera records green, pink or purple.

Northern and eastern Quebec offer particularly favourable viewing conditions because of their latitude, dark skies and open horizons. Aurora can also be seen farther south during stronger geomagnetic activity. Along the Côte-Nord and in Gaspésie, observers may see displays over the St Lawrence where low horizons and limited light pollution make faint structures more noticeable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.quebecmaritime.ca/en/blog/northern-lights-when-the-sky-dances-over-eastern-quebec" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quebecmaritime.ca">[Québec maritime]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quebecmaritime.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">northern lights when the sky dances over eastern quebec</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">northern lights when the sky dances over eastern quebec</span></span></span>

Auroral forms can change in ways that resemble descriptions of enormous aerial objects. Rays may brighten one after another, creating apparent sideways motion. An arc can remain nearly stationary while ripples pass through it. Diffuse aurora may form a broad luminous area with darker gaps that witnesses interpret as the outline of a body. Canadian tourism guidance accurately describes aurora as taking the form of arcs, rippling curtains, mist-like wisps and translucent clouds, with features capable of moving surprisingly quickly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://travel.destinationcanada.com/en-gb/things-to-do/where-see-spectacular-northern-lights-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: travel.destinationcanada.com">[Destination Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">travel.destinationcanada.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on destinationcanada.com.</span></span></span>

Aurora is less persuasive as an explanation when a report describes a compact object below clouds, a light passing directly in front of terrain or a sharply bounded form moving against the stars. It is strongest when the observation covers a large part of the northern sky, changes shape rather than following one continuous track, and coincides with known geomagnetic activity.

## Why remote skies distort size and speed

A dark sky provides excellent visibility but poor distance information. In daylight, the brain estimates range using texture, haze, shadows, overlapping objects and familiar landmarks. At night, a light may have none of those cues. A dim nearby lamp, a bright aircraft many kilometres away and a planet millions of kilometres away can all occupy a similar apparent angle.

This creates the central measurement problem in many Quebec UFO accounts: apparent size cannot be converted into physical size unless distance is known. A witness who says a light was “as large as a house” may be describing its visual impression, not a measured object. If the assumed distance is doubled, the inferred size also changes dramatically.

Remote roads, lakes and northern settlements can make the problem more severe. A light seen across water or above an unbroken treeline lacks reference points. The observer may interpret the movement of their own vehicle, boat or head as movement by the light. Transport Canada’s discussion of night illusions is relevant beyond aviation: stationary objects can appear to move, ground lights can be confused with stars, and even the northern lights may provide a misleading apparent horizon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/publications/handbook-civil-aviation-medical-examiners-tp-13312" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada Handbook for Civil Aviation Medical Examiners</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When watched intently they will appear to move and may be mistaken for other aircraft.This movement of stationary objects is known as aut...</span></span></span>

Cloud gaps introduce another illusion. A star or planet visible through moving cloud may seem to accelerate in the opposite direction. Successive openings can make one stationary object appear at several positions. When the cloud itself is invisible in darkness, the observer sees only a light that darts, dims and returns.

## What separates a plausible explanation from a guess?

Calling every unexplained light “Venus” or “ice crystals” is no more rigorous than calling it an alien craft. A useful natural explanation must match the details of the report.

Investigators should ask whether the proposed cause was in the correct direction and at the correct time; whether the duration fits; whether weather conditions support cloud or ice effects; and whether other observers reported a matching event. Astronomical software can check planets and bright stars. Meteor networks can compare exact times and viewing directions. Weather records can establish cloud height, visibility and temperature. Geomagnetic records can test an aurora hypothesis.

The strongest natural identifications usually have a distinctive signature:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Planet or star:** nearly fixed position, long duration, no sound, low in the evening or morning sky.
* **Meteor or fireball:** seconds rather than minutes, a continuous path, sudden flares or fragmentation, reports spread across a wide area.
* **Aurora:** broad structures, changing curtains or rays, strongest towards the north, often recorded from several communities.
* **Ice-crystal display:** pillars, arcs or repeated geometric lights during cold weather, aligned with the Moon or terrestrial illumination.
* **Cloud illumination:** diffuse patches tied to a cloud base, often changing as beams or clouds move.

</div>

A report remains unresolved when the available evidence cannot discriminate between these possibilities. That status records a limitation in the investigation; it is not a finding that natural causes failed. Quebec’s skies provide enough powerful astronomical and atmospheric effects to explain many apparently extraordinary observations, while also showing why incomplete accounts can remain mysterious long after the light itself has disappeared.
