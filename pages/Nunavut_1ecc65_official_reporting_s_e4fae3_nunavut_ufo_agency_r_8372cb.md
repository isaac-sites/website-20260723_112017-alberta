## Introduction

A single unusual sighting over [Nunavut]({{ 'what-really-lies-behind-nunavuts-ufo/' | relative_url }}) can generate very different official responses because Canada does not have one agency responsible for investigating UFO or unidentified aerial phenomenon (UAP) reports. Instead, each organisation responds only if the report falls within its legal mandate. The same event might therefore be logged by air traffic services as an aviation occurrence, assessed by defence authorities as a possible airspace-security issue, noted by police if public safety is involved, or receive no further investigation at all if none of those thresholds is met.

This division of responsibility often surprises members of the public. Different agencies are not necessarily disagreeing about what witnesses saw. More commonly, they are answering different questions. One organisation asks whether aircraft were endangered, another whether Canadian airspace may have been violated, and another whether a crime or emergency has occurred. In Nunavut, where most unusual sightings occur against a backdrop of remote communities, sparse radar coverage and heavy reliance on aviation, these distinctions are especially important.

## What each agency is responsible for

The agencies most likely to encounter a Nunavut sighting have separate statutory roles rather than a shared UFO investigation programme.

**NAV CANADA** is responsible for the safe movement of civil aircraft. If a [pilot reports]({{ 'pilot-reports/' | relative_url }}) an unidentified object through normal air traffic communications or the CIRVIS reporting procedure, controllers record operational details and, where appropriate, generate an aviation occurrence report. The concern is whether aircraft separation, flight safety or air traffic operations were affected—not whether the object represents an unexplained phenomenon.

**Transport Canada** oversees civil aviation regulation. Reports received through systems such as the Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS) become part of an aviation safety database that helps identify hazards and operational trends. A CADORS entry documents an occurrence but is not an official determination that something extraordinary was present.

The **Transportation Safety Board of Canada (TSB)** has an even narrower role. It investigates selected transportation accidents and incidents to improve safety, not to identify unusual aerial objects. The Board explicitly states that it investigates only a proportion of reported occurrences according to their potential safety value, meaning many reports receive only limited recording rather than a full investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bst.gc.ca/eng/lois-acts/evenements-occurrences.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bst.gc.ca">[Transportation Safety Board of Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bst.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transportation Safety Board of Canada Policy on Occurrence Classification</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transportation Safety Board of CanadaPolicy on Occurrence Classification - Transportation Safety Board of CanadaApril 5, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 5, 2023</span></span></span>

**NORAD**, the binational North American Aerospace Defense Command, has a defence rather than scientific mission. It monitors North American airspace for potential military or security threats. An unidentified object only becomes a NORAD concern if sensors or operational circumstances suggest it could affect continental defence, sovereign airspace or military operations.

The **Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP)** or local police become involved only when a report suggests criminal activity, an emergency, public safety risks or the need to assist another agency. Simply seeing an unusual light in the sky is generally outside normal policing responsibilities.

## When a sighting becomes a safety or security matter

The same observation can move through several official systems because each evaluates different risks.

For example, imagine a flight crew approaching Iqaluit reports bright lights manoeuvring near its route. NAV CANADA's immediate concern is preventing a collision or maintaining safe aircraft separation. Transport Canada may later record the occurrence in CADORS because it affected aviation operations. If radar or other sensors suggest an unknown aircraft has entered controlled airspace, military authorities could separately assess whether national security questions arise. If the object turns out to be a weather balloon, satellite reflection or another identifiable source, each organisation may simply close its own part of the matter without any wider investigation.

By contrast, if several residents report unusual lights over a remote Nunavut community but no aircraft are involved, no radar detects an unknown target and no crime is suspected, none of those agencies may have a reason to conduct an extensive inquiry. The reports may remain local accounts rather than becoming major government investigations.

This explains why members of the public sometimes perceive official responses as inconsistent. The agencies are applying different legal tests rather than competing explanations.

## Why many reports receive little follow-up

Many Nunavut sightings never progress beyond an initial report because they do not meet the threshold for further action.

Several practical factors contribute:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **No single Canadian UFO office.** Canada has no permanent agency whose sole purpose is investigating every unidentified aerial sighting.
* **Resource priorities.** Agencies investigate issues that directly relate to their statutory responsibilities, such as aviation accidents, defence threats or criminal activity.
* **Limited evidence.** Remote locations often mean there are few photographs, radar returns or independent witnesses to compare.
* **No continuing operational risk.** If an object disappears without affecting aircraft, infrastructure or public safety, there may be little justification for additional government resources.
* **Plausible conventional explanations.** Weather phenomena, astronomical objects, satellites, balloons and ordinary aircraft frequently account for reports once additional information becomes available.

</div>

The Transportation Safety Board follows a formal occurrence-classification system that allows many reported events to receive only limited data collection when they are unlikely to produce new transportation safety lessons. This reflects the Board's safety mandate rather than any judgement about whether witnesses were sincere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tsb.gc.ca/eng/lois-acts/evenements-occurrences.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tsb.gc.ca">[Transportation Safety Board of Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tsb.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transportation Safety Board of Canada Policy on Occurrence Classification</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transportation Safety Board of CanadaPolicy on Occurrence Classification - Transportation Safety Board of CanadaApril 5, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 5, 2023</span></span></span>

## Why different official conclusions are not necessarily contradictory

One agency may describe an event as an "unidentified object" while another effectively closes the file without further comment. These outcomes can appear contradictory but usually are not.

In aviation, "unidentified" often means only that the observer could not identify the object at the time of the report. It does not imply an extraordinary explanation. Likewise, a defence organisation may determine that an object poses no security threat without determining exactly what it was, while a transportation authority may conclude that the event produced no safety lessons worth a detailed investigation.

Canadian law also anticipates overlapping responsibilities. The Transportation Safety Board's governing legislation makes clear that its investigations do not prevent other departments, including the RCMP, from conducting their own investigations for purposes within their authority. Each organisation therefore reaches conclusions relevant to its own mandate rather than issuing a single national verdict on the sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://laws.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-23.4/page-2.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: laws.justice.gc.ca">[Department of Justice Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">laws.justice.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of Justice CanadaCanadian Transportation Accident Investigation and Safety Board ActMay 28, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 28, 2026</span></span></span>

## What this means for Nunavut reports

Nunavut's geography reinforces this fragmented approach. The territory depends heavily on aviation, has vast sparsely populated areas and sits beneath strategically important northern airspace. As a result, a pilot's report is more likely to enter official systems than a civilian observation from the ground because aviation reporting procedures are well established.

Even so, the existence of multiple reporting pathways should not be mistaken for evidence that governments are conducting parallel UFO investigations. Most agencies simply assess whether a reported event falls within their operational responsibilities. If it does not present an aviation hazard, transportation occurrence, security concern or policing matter, the report may receive little additional attention despite remaining unexplained from the witness's perspective.

Understanding these differing mandates helps explain why one Nunavut sighting may appear in aviation databases, prompt military awareness, receive no police action and never become the subject of a comprehensive government investigation—all without any disagreement over the basic facts of the original report.
