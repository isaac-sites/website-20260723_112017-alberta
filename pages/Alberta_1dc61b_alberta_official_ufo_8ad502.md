## Introduction

[Alberta]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-albertas-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s government UFO files prove that unusual aerial reports were received, recorded and sometimes investigated by Canadian authorities. They do not prove that extraterrestrial craft visited the province. The surviving records show officials checking possible threats to aviation, defence, public safety and scientific understanding, often with limited evidence and overlapping responsibilities. Library and Archives Canada holds roughly 9,500 federal UFO-related documents accumulated from 1947 to the early 1980s, including material from National Defence, Transport, the National Research Council and the Royal Canadian Mounted Police. Alberta locations appear throughout that collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

The distinction matters. An official file confirms that a report entered government paperwork; an investigation confirms that somebody attempted to assess it. Neither action validates the witness’s interpretation. Even the word “unresolved” normally means that the available information was insufficient for a confident identification—not that investigators eliminated every ordinary explanation and were left with an alien spacecraft.

## Which Canadian agencies kept Alberta UFO records?

Canada never had one continuous, central UFO agency comparable to the popular image of a secret national investigation office. Instead, reports moved through a changing network of departments whose interest depended on what the incident appeared to involve.

Library and Archives Canada identifies four principal federal sources for its historical UFO collection:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The Department of National Defence**, including the Royal Canadian Air Force and defence research establishments, handled reports that might affect airspace, military installations or national security.
* **The Department of Transport** received material connected with aircraft, air-traffic operations and potentially hazardous objects in controlled airspace.
* **The National Research Council** assessed many reports through scientific and astronomical expertise, including records associated with its meteor and astrophysics work.
* **The Royal Canadian Mounted Police** recorded or investigated incidents reported to local detachments, particularly where there were possible safety, property or policing concerns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

</div>

This division of labour explains why one Alberta incident can generate several documents without representing several independent confirmations. A civilian might contact the police; the police might notify defence authorities; a military officer might request scientific advice; and copies of the same correspondence might later be filed by more than one body. The resulting paper trail can look impressive while still resting on a single original observation.

The federal archive itself warns researchers about these limitations. Some records are undated, only about half identify a definite sighting location, and searches by place or date return incomplete results because catalogue information reproduces what appeared on the original documents. A count of records mentioning Alberta therefore cannot be treated as a complete total of Alberta sightings, let alone a measure of unexplained activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

## Why officials collected UFO reports

Government interest was usually practical rather than extraterrestrial. An unexplained light could be an aircraft in distress, an unidentified balloon, an experimental vehicle, a meteor, a radar anomaly, a foreign intrusion or a misunderstanding by a member of the public. During the Cold War, Canadian officials could not safely assume that an unknown object was harmless simply because its identity was unclear.

The records also served an administrative purpose. Officials needed standard forms, correspondence procedures and places to forward reports. Some documents in the federal collection concern individual sightings, but others are instructions, reporting templates and internal memoranda. Their existence shows that government employees had procedures for handling the subject; it does not show that the government endorsed the claims contained in every form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

Modern aviation records work in much the same way. Transport Canada’s Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System, known as CADORS, exists to collect preliminary information about aviation occurrences, identify hazards and notify relevant organisations. Reports can come from NAV CANADA, police, aircraft operators, the Transportation Safety Board and other agencies. CADORS entries may be corrected or expanded as further information becomes available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/publications/aviation-safety-letter/issue-2-2021/civil-aviation-daily-occurrence-reporting-system-cadors" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada The Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada The Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS</span></span></span>

Transport Canada explicitly cautions that “UFO” in an aviation record can describe drones, balloons, meteors, birds or weather phenomena. It should not be read as shorthand for an extraterrestrial object. Depending on the circumstances, follow-up may involve Transport Canada, NAV CANADA, the Transportation Safety Board or the North American Aerospace Defence Command, commonly known as NORAD.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/binder/4-high-altitude-object-incidents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada4. Alberta’s police experience offers a smaller-scale example. RCMP emergency operators reported receiving calls about strange lights and apparent UFOs during the pandemic, including sightings that dispatchers could identify as lines of SpaceX satellites. Their immediate concern was not proving or disproving alien visitation but deciding whether anyone faced a genuine emergency, aviation issue, break-in or other police matter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://globalnews.ca/news/7827693/alberta-rcmp-ufo-sightings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalnews.ca">[Global News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalnews.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on globalnews.ca.</span></span></span>

## Duhamel shows both the value and the limits of an official file

The most useful Alberta example is the 1967 investigation of circular ground marks near [Duhamel]({{ 'duhamel/' | relative_url }}), close to Camrose. Unlike many sighting reports, the case involved physical features that an investigator could visit, measure and photograph. That makes the file more substantial than a brief telephone account—but still much less conclusive than it is sometimes presented.

On 11 August 1967, G. H. S. Jones of the Defence Research Establishment Suffield was instructed by Defence Research Board headquarters to inspect the marks. An army officer and photographer also attended. Jones’s memorandum records the travel arrangements, local accounts, dimensions and appearance of the impressions. It therefore establishes that a real site existed and that Canadian defence personnel examined it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.experiencersupport.org/declassified/onsite-inspection-of-ufo-landing-marks-at-duhamel-camrose-county-in-alberta" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: experiencersupport.org">[Experiencer Support]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">experiencersupport.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on experiencersupport.org.</span></span></span>

The document also contains warnings that are often lost in retellings. Jones said that his summary of local stories should not be taken as his judgement of the witnesses’ truthfulness or reliability. He noted that one description attributed to two local girls resembled accounts already common in published UFO stories. Most importantly, nobody was documented as seeing an object create the rings in the field. The owner found them after visiting an area of pasture he had not inspected for about a week, so even the time of their formation was uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.experiencersupport.org/declassified/onsite-inspection-of-ufo-landing-marks-at-duhamel-camrose-county-in-alberta" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: experiencersupport.org">[Experiencer Support]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">experiencersupport.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on experiencersupport.org.</span></span></span>

That gap breaks the chain of evidence. The file contains unusual marks and separate reports of unusual objects in the district, but it does not demonstrate that the same event caused both. Connecting them requires an inference, not a recorded observation.

Some popular accounts highlight the investigator’s calculations about the large force or weight that might have been needed to produce the depressions. Such passages show that he explored mechanical possibilities. They are not a finding that a 135-ton spacecraft landed there. A hypothetical estimate based on pressure, dimensions and assumed contact conditions does not identify the machine responsible, and the document contains no recovered vehicle, manufactured fragment, propulsion residue or instrument reading establishing a craft of unknown origin.

Duhamel therefore remains legitimately puzzling. The official file strengthens the case that the marks were not simply invented after the fact, because trained personnel documented them. It does not strengthen the extraterrestrial interpretation to the same degree. The central evidential weakness—a missing eyewitness to the marks being made—was never repaired.

## How to read an “unresolved” government file

“Unresolved” is an administrative and evidential category, not a positive explanation. It usually means that investigators could not identify an object or cause from the available material. Several very different situations can produce that outcome:

* The report arrived too late to collect physical or atmospheric data.
* The witness supplied only a short description or uncertain time.
* No photograph, radar track or second observation existed.
* Relevant aircraft, balloon or astronomical records were unavailable.
* Agencies completed only the checks needed for safety or security.
* The event was too minor to justify further expenditure.
* Plausible [explanations]({{ 'explanations/' | relative_url }}) existed, but none could be demonstrated conclusively.

An unresolved file can therefore be weaker than an explained one, not stronger. A detailed report may permit investigators to identify Venus, a meteor, a satellite formation or an aircraft. A vague report of a fast-moving light may remain unidentified because it contains too little information to test.

The federal Sky Canada Project reached a similar conclusion about the present reporting system. Launched by the Office of the Chief Science Advisor, it examined how public reports of unidentified aerial phenomena are managed, rather than attempting to determine what the phenomena are. Its findings describe fragmented responsibilities, inconsistent data and a lack of systematic national collection. The project expressly separated improving public reporting from proving or denying extraterrestrial visitation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/preview-sky-canada-report-ocsa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">preview sky canada report ocsa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaPreview: Sky Canada Report from the Office of the Chief...Jan 15, 2025 — This report focuses on the services available to the...</span></span></span>

For Alberta, this means that the absence of a final explanation in an old military, police or scientific file should not be overstated. It may reflect a genuinely unusual event, but it may equally reflect lost context, poor reporting or an investigation designed only to decide whether immediate official action was required.

## What the files genuinely establish

Read carefully, Alberta’s official UFO records support several firm conclusions.

**People repeatedly reported unusual things in Alberta’s skies.** These reports reached police, defence, transport and scientific authorities over several decades. The archive is evidence of a persistent reporting phenomenon, not merely later folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

**Officials sometimes took reports seriously enough to investigate.** The [Duhamel inspection]({{ 'duhamel-file/' | relative_url }}) involved defence researchers, military transport, an army officer and photography. That level of response shows institutional interest in determining whether the marks had an ordinary, hazardous or defence-related cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.experiencersupport.org/declassified/onsite-inspection-of-ufo-landing-marks-at-duhamel-camrose-county-in-alberta" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: experiencersupport.org">[Experiencer Support]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">experiencersupport.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on experiencersupport.org.</span></span></span>

**“UFO” was a descriptive category.** It meant that an object or event had not yet been identified by the observer or recorder. Transport Canada continues to use comparable terminology for a wide range of airborne objects and natural effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/binder/4-high-altitude-object-incidents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada4. High Altitude Object Incidents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada4. High Altitude Object Incidents</span></span></span>

**Many records were created for safety, intelligence or administrative reasons.** Authorities did not need to believe in alien visitors before recording something that might interfere with aircraft, alarm the public or indicate an unknown object in Canadian airspace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/publications/aviation-safety-letter/issue-2-2021/civil-aviation-daily-occurrence-reporting-system-cadors" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada The Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada The Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS</span></span></span>

**Some Alberta incidents were never conclusively explained.** Duhamel is the clearest historical example. Its unresolved status is real, but it is a statement about the limits of the surviving evidence—not a verified explanation of the marks.

## What the files do not prove

The records do not establish that Canadian authorities recovered alien technology, confirmed extraterrestrial occupants or secretly classified Alberta sightings as spacecraft encounters. No publicly available Alberta file supplies the chain of evidence that such a conclusion would require: independently verified sensor data, recoverable material with demonstrably non-human manufacture, reliable observations of an object producing the alleged effects, and analysis open to replication.

Nor does the size of the archive demonstrate a concealed discovery. The approximately 9,500 digitised documents include duplicated correspondence, administrative material, procedures and reports lacking definite dates or locations. Volume reflects decades of record-keeping across several agencies, not thousands of confirmed anomalous craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

Official attention should not be confused with official endorsement. Governments investigate uncertain reports precisely because their cause is not yet known. Police record allegations that are later unsubstantiated; aviation authorities record preliminary occurrences that are corrected; defence organisations check unidentified objects because some may be ordinary but operationally important. UFO files belong to the same evidential world.

## The most defensible conclusion

Alberta’s government UFO files are valuable because they preserve what witnesses said, what officials did and where investigations reached their limits. They show that the subject was not simply ignored or laughed away. In selected cases, authorities interviewed people, visited locations, consulted technical specialists and retained the results.

Their strongest finding, however, is modest: unidentified events occurred in the sense that some reports could not be confidently resolved. The documents do not move logically from “unidentified” to “extraterrestrial”. Duhamel remains an interesting physical-trace case because the marks were documented and their origin was not established, but the file lacks a witnessed landing or decisive material evidence.

The official record therefore supports neither blanket dismissal nor claims of confirmed alien visitation. It supports a narrower and more useful position: Alberta produced reports worth recording, a few cases worth investigating, many ordinary or probable explanations, and a small residue whose uncertainty survived because the evidence did not.
