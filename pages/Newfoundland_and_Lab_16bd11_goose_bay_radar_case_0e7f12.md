## Introduction

The Goose Bay radar incident of 1948 is often presented as an early military detection of an unknown flying object. The surviving file supports a more cautious conclusion. Two ground-controlled approach radar operators apparently watched a distinct return move across a short-range display for several minutes, but the reported position, route, distance and disappearance point could not all be reconciled. The case remained unidentified largely because the measurements were contradictory and the available records were insufficient—not because investigators established extraordinary performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/goose-bay-labrador-october-1948-incident-number-illegible-28931625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">goose bay labrador october 1948 incident number illegible 28931625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: Goose Bay, Labrador, October 1948 - Incident Number: [ILLEGIBLE] · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: october 1948</span></span></span>

That distinction makes Goose Bay important within the UFO history of [Newfoundland and Labrador]({{ 'newfoundland-and-labrador/' | relative_url }}). It shows how a real radar observation can become a durable mystery when rough readings are later treated as precise flight data. The most defensible verdict is therefore **unresolved but weakly measured**, with a balloon, an equipment or propagation effect, or an ordinary target still plausible.

## What radar operators recorded in 1948

The incident occurred at Goose Bay in Labrador, then a strategically important North Atlantic airfield used by Canadian and United States forces. Contemporary records entered the United States Air Force reporting system, explaining why the surviving material is held in the US National Archives rather than solely in Canadian files. Goose Bay’s military importance and international operations made an unidentified radar contact worth documenting even when there was no suggestion of an extraterrestrial craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/goose-bay-labrador-october-1948-incident-number-illegible-28931625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">goose bay labrador october 1948 incident number illegible 28931625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: Goose Bay, Labrador, October 1948 - Incident Number: [ILLEGIBLE] · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: october 1948</span></span></span>

There is some confusion over the date. The archival case is catalogued under October 1948, while its surviving summary places the observation on the evening of 1 November. Older UFO literature has also used 29 or 31 October. The discrepancy is a warning against building a minute-by-minute reconstruction from later retellings. The safest description is that the event occurred around the end of October or beginning of November 1948.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/goose-bay-labrador-october-1948-incident-number-illegible-28931625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">goose bay labrador october 1948 incident number illegible 28931625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: Goose Bay, Labrador, October 1948 - Incident Number: [ILLEGIBLE] · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: october 1948</span></span></span>

According to the case-file summary, a target appeared at about 9.45 p.m., initially plotted roughly 3¾ miles north-east of the radar station. It remained visible for a little over four minutes and was estimated to have travelled approximately four miles at about 60 miles per hour before disappearing from a display set to a range of 7½ miles. Two ground-controlled approach, or GCA, operators observed it. GCA radar was designed primarily to help controllers guide aircraft during approaches, not to conduct a scientific study of unidentified aerial objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/goose-bay-labrador-october-1948-incident-number-illegible-28931625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">goose bay labrador october 1948 incident number illegible 28931625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: Goose Bay, Labrador, October 1948 - Incident Number: [ILLEGIBLE] · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: october 1948</span></span></span>

No corresponding object was reported visually. Nor does the surviving file provide photographs of the scope, an automatic track, independently timed plots from separate radar stations, a recovered object or a confirming aircraft interception. Claims sometimes attached to other Goose Bay stories—such as extreme altitude or speeds of thousands of miles per hour—should not be merged with this particular short-range event. The measurements in this file describe a comparatively slow target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/goose-bay-labrador-october-1948-incident-number-illegible-28931625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">goose bay labrador october 1948 incident number illegible 28931625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: Goose Bay, Labrador, October 1948 - Incident Number: [ILLEGIBLE] · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: october 1948</span></span></span>

## Why the plotted route did not add up

The strongest evidence in the case is also its central weakness: the operators recorded a moving return, but investigators could not turn the reported figures into a coherent track. When Newfoundland Base Command personnel plotted the stated starting position, direction and disappearance point, the geometry did not work. The object’s quoted speed and distance would also have placed it somewhere different from the bearings at which it was reportedly lost. These problems were explicitly raised during the follow-up in January 1949.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/goose-bay-labrador-october-1948-incident-number-illegible-28931625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">goose bay labrador october 1948 incident number illegible 28931625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: Goose Bay, Labrador, October 1948 - Incident Number: [ILLEGIBLE] · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: october 1948</span></span></span>

This is more serious than a minor rounding error. Range, bearing, elapsed time and direction are linked measurements. If one or more were copied incorrectly, estimated from memory or taken from different moments in the observation, calculations of the target’s route and behaviour become unreliable. A radar return can be genuine while the reconstructed journey attributed to it is not.

The reported size judgement also deserves caution. The operator apparently considered the echo too small to represent an aircraft but too large to be a bird. Primary radar, however, does not display a direct silhouette of an object. It displays reflected radio energy, whose apparent strength and shape can vary with the target’s material, orientation, distance, weather conditions and the radar’s settings. A larger or brighter mark on a scope is not a straightforward measurement of physical dimensions. Modern aviation guidance still distinguishes a displayed radar “target” from a confirmed identification of the object producing it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap4_section_5.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

Nor did [two operators]({{ 'two-operators/' | relative_url }}) watching the same display provide two independent instrument detections. Their agreement supports the claim that a return appeared on the scope, but both observers were dependent on the same radar, calibration, atmospheric path and display. Independent confirmation would have required another radar operating from a different position, a simultaneous visual observation, an aircraft report or another sensor.

The case’s later reputation has sometimes reversed this evidential problem. Because the figures cannot be reconciled, the return is described as performing inexplicable manoeuvres. A better reading is that the alleged manoeuvres cannot be established. Contradictory measurements do not demonstrate extraordinary motion; they prevent reliable motion from being calculated.

## Balloons, equipment effects and insufficient data

A weather balloon or rawinsonde was considered during the original investigation. A rawinsonde carries instruments that transmit atmospheric measurements while its movement allows winds aloft to be calculated. The recorded ground wind at Goose Bay was reportedly from the west at roughly 5 to 10 miles per hour, while the radar target seemed to move against it, leading investigators to regard a balloon as doubtful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/goose-bay-labrador-october-1948-incident-number-illegible-28931625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">goose bay labrador october 1948 incident number illegible 28931625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: Goose Bay, Labrador, October 1948 - Incident Number: [ILLEGIBLE] · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: october 1948</span></span></span>

That objection did not conclusively eliminate the balloon possibility. Surface wind does not necessarily match the wind at a balloon’s altitude, and the contradictory route data make the claimed direction uncertain in the first place. The file, as now available, does not preserve enough altitude information or a detailed upper-air wind profile to test the explanation properly. A balloon is therefore plausible rather than proven.

Radar-related effects are another possibility, although no single fault can now be demonstrated. Radar beams may bend under unusual atmospheric conditions and generate misleading or extraneous echoes, a phenomenon known as anomalous propagation. Terrain, reflections, clutter and equipment behaviour can also produce targets whose apparent locations or movements do not correspond neatly to a single aircraft or physical object. These are recognised limitations of radar generally, but applying one of them to Goose Bay requires environmental and technical records that the surviving case does not supply.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/AIM/aim0405.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[faa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

An ordinary aircraft, bird formation or other airborne material cannot be securely identified either. The operators believed the return differed from familiar traffic, but there was no preserved secondary identification signal, visual check or complete traffic record available in the public file. Operator experience is relevant evidence, yet it cannot repair missing or mutually inconsistent measurements.

The appropriate classification is therefore close to the archival description: **insufficient data**. This does not mean that nothing appeared on the radar, nor that the operators invented the observation. It means the surviving information cannot distinguish confidently between a balloon, an ordinary airborne target, a radar artefact and a genuinely unusual object. The official record documents uncertainty; it does not resolve that uncertainty in favour of an exotic explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/goose-bay-labrador-october-1948-incident-number-illegible-28931625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">goose bay labrador october 1948 incident number illegible 28931625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: Goose Bay, Labrador, October 1948 - Incident Number: [ILLEGIBLE] · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: october 1948</span></span></span>

## What the case contributes to Newfoundland and Labrador’s UFO history

Goose Bay remains notable because it joins military personnel, radar equipment and an early official UFO file at one of Labrador’s most important airfields. It also predates Newfoundland’s entry into Canadian Confederation on 31 March 1949, which helps explain the mixture of Newfoundland, Canadian and American institutional history surrounding the report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govweird.com/topics/ufo/project-blue-book/goose-bay-labrador-october-1948-incident-number-illegible-28931625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govweird.com">[govweird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govweird.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">goose bay labrador october 1948 incident number illegible 28931625</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book: Goose Bay, Labrador, October 1948 - Incident Number: [ILLEGIBLE] · govweird...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: october 1948</span></span></span>

Its real lesson, however, is methodological. Radar is often assumed to provide automatic proof of distance, speed and physical reality. In practice, a convincing case requires consistent plots, known instrument performance, precise timing, environmental data and preferably independent sensors. Modern technical proposals for studying unidentified aerial phenomena likewise emphasise multiple instruments and cross-checking because one sensor can produce artefacts or ambiguous readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The Goose Bay target was never satisfactorily identified, but “unidentified” is not the same as “inexplicable”. Later access to the file has weakened the dramatic version of the story by exposing the mathematical contradictions within the original report. What survives is a credible account of an uncertain radar return—and a useful example of how measurement flaws can preserve an ordinary event as a mystery for decades.
