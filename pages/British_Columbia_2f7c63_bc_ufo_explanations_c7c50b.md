## Introduction

Most [British Columbia]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-british/' | relative_url }}) UFO reports are eventually explained, or at least narrowed down, by checking ordinary objects and viewing conditions: aircraft, bright planets, meteors, satellites, balloons, drones, lanterns, reflections and atmospheric effects. The province’s mountains, coastlines and dark rural skies can make these familiar things look unfamiliar because [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses/' | relative_url }}) often lack reliable clues to distance, height, scale and speed.

The remaining cases are not automatically evidence of extraordinary craft. Frequently, they remain unresolved because the report lacks an exact time, direction, duration, photograph, original video or independent corroboration. ## Astronomical and aviation causes

A useful investigation begins by asking what should have been visible from the witness’s location at the stated time. That means comparing the report with astronomical charts, aircraft movements, satellite passes, weather records and known launches. The strongest explanations reproduce several features at once—position, direction, brightness, duration and apparent movement—rather than merely naming something that could have been in the sky.

**Bright planets and stars** account for many reports of lights that hover, flash colours or seem to follow a vehicle. Venus is especially conspicuous near sunset or before dawn and may appear larger or closer than expected. Turbulence in the lower atmosphere can make a low star or planet shimmer, change colour or appear to move slightly. UFO*BC, a civilian group that publishes provincial reports, has repeatedly identified submitted sightings as probable Venus observations, including cases in which the time and reported movement fitted the planet’s position. Other archived British Columbia reports have been attributed to Venus and Jupiter together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufobc.ca/Sightings/sights2001_v2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufobc.ca">[UFO*BC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufobc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sights2001 v2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 2001, Vancouver, BC: This sighting was one that I had in 2001, during the summer around July...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2001</span></span></span>

The apparent movement of a stationary celestial object can be surprisingly convincing. A witness in a moving car has trees, poles and mountain ridges passing through the foreground, while the distant light remains in roughly the same direction. The result can feel as though the light is keeping pace. When the vehicle turns, the object may seem to turn as well. Without nearby reference points, ordinary eye movements and drifting cloud can also create the impression that a point of light has darted or swayed.

**Aircraft** become difficult to recognise when seen head-on, at night or through broken cloud. A landing light aimed towards an observer can look like a brilliant stationary orb because there is little visible sideways motion. As the aircraft turns, the light may suddenly dim, disappear or separate into red, green and white navigation lights. UFO*BC has used this explanation for a Surrey-area observation that was consistent with an aircraft approaching Vancouver International Airport before turning on to its final approach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufobc.ca/Sightings/sights2004_v2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufobc.ca">[UFO*BC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufobc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sights2004 v2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">&gt;&gt; Calls came in from Kelowna, Westbridge and Grand Forks, British Columbia after a blue colored object was observed traveling in a south...</span></span></span>

British Columbia has several busy and overlapping aviation environments: Vancouver International Airport, Victoria International Airport, Abbotsford International Airport, military activity around Canadian Forces Base Comox, helicopter traffic, floatplanes, medevac flights and regional routes threading through valleys. An aircraft that appears to fly towards a mountain may actually be many kilometres in front of it or behind it. Its silence is not decisive either. Sound arrives later than light, and wind, terrain, insulated buildings and distance can make even a large aircraft inaudible.

Aircraft explanations should nevertheless be demonstrated rather than assumed. Public flight-tracking services do not show every military, police, helicopter or private movement, and their historical coverage varies. A convincing match needs the correct place, time, direction and pattern of motion. Simply noting that an airport lies somewhere nearby is not enough.

## Satellites, meteors and newer sources of confusion

Artificial satellites have changed the appearance of the night sky. A single satellite normally resembles a steady star moving smoothly across the background. It may brighten as its surfaces reflect sunlight and then vanish abruptly when it enters the Earth’s shadow. This behaviour can be misread as acceleration, disappearance or an object “switching off”.

SpaceX Starlink launches created a particularly recognisable new class of UFO report: a line or train of evenly spaced lights crossing the sky. UFO*BC identified reported formations over British Columbia as Starlink satellites in 2019 and 2020, including an observation for which the predicted pass time matched visibility over Vancouver.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufobc.ca/Sightings/sights2019_v2.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufobc.ca">[UFO*BC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufobc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sights2019 v2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO*BCUFO SIGHTING REPORTS FROM 2019July 23, 2020 — UFO*BC Responds: What you saw were the Starlink satellites deployed by Spacex...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 23, 2020</span></span></span>

Not every satellite observation is a neat train. Recently deployed spacecraft spread apart over time, while perspective can make a formation look compressed, curved or clustered. Several satellites may brighten in succession, producing the impression that lights are appearing from one point. Tumbling rocket bodies and other orbiting objects can also fluctuate dramatically. An adequate check therefore requires the precise date, time, viewing direction and location rather than a general comparison with photographs of Starlink.

**Meteors and fireballs** explain many short, fast sightings, especially reports involving a luminous object, coloured trail, fragmentation or apparent descent behind a ridge. A meteor can appear green, blue, orange or white depending on brightness, composition, atmospheric effects and the observer’s vision. Because it is seen against the distant sky, a witness may believe it fell into a nearby town, lake or forest when its actual path was hundreds of kilometres away.

British Columbia archives contain repeated descriptions of green or blue fireballs over places such as Victoria, the Fraser Valley and the southern Interior. Some were reported across widely separated communities at similar times, a pattern much more consistent with a high-altitude meteor visible across the Pacific Northwest than with a low object travelling over one neighbourhood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufobc.ca/Sightings/sights2000_v2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufobc.ca">[UFO*BC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufobc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sights2000 v2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sights2000 v2</span></span></span>

Duration is an important discriminator. A brilliant event lasting only a few seconds, moving continuously in one direction and perhaps breaking apart is likely to be a meteor. A light that remains visible for minutes is more likely to be an aircraft, satellite, lantern, drone or astronomical object. Witness estimates of time are often inaccurate during a startling event, so investigators look for videos, clocks, phone records and reports from other locations.

Other human-made objects fill the gaps between these familiar categories. Weather and telecommunications balloons may drift slowly or seem motionless. Recreational drones can change direction sharply and display unusual lighting. Sky lanterns commonly appear as orange lights moving together with the wind before fading one by one. The Sky Canada report lists aircraft, balloons, drones, satellites, lanterns and experimental aircraft among the ordinary sources that observers may initially classify as unidentified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.gc.ca">[science.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s or experimental aircraft can also be classified as UAPs by observers...</span></span></span>

## How terrain and weather distort perception

British Columbia’s geography does not create UFOs, but it often removes the visual information needed to identify an object. Much of the province is made up of mountains, forest, islands, inlets and large stretches of water. In such settings, a witness may see a light without any object of known size beside it. Once distance is unknown, estimates of size and speed become highly uncertain.

A light moving slowly nearby and a much brighter light travelling quickly far away can cross the same apparent angle in the sky. This is why reports that an object was “as large as a house” or crossed a valley at extreme speed cannot be treated as measurements unless its distance was independently established. A mountain skyline can add false certainty: an object appearing just above a ridge may be over the ridge, far beyond it or well in front of it.

Valleys can also hide ordinary aircraft for part of their route. A light may emerge suddenly from behind a slope, disappear into cloud and reappear elsewhere after a turn. On the coast, layers of low cloud and fog can conceal an aircraft’s body while allowing its lights to shine through or reflect from the cloud base. Ice crystals, temperature inversions, storms and cloud layers can distort, scatter or multiply light, all of which are specifically recognised in the Sky Canada assessment as possible sources of unusual aerial appearances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.gc.ca">[science.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s or experimental aircraft can also be classified as UAPs by observers...</span></span></span>

**Atmospheric scintillation** is especially important for reports of multicoloured flashing. Light from a star or planet near the horizon passes through more turbulent atmosphere than light from an object overhead. Small changes in air density bend different wavelengths unevenly, producing rapid red, blue, green or white flashes. Binoculars or a phone’s digital zoom may enlarge the blur without resolving the source, making it look more structured rather than less.

**Reflections and camera artefacts** form another large category. A bright interior lamp reflected in a window can appear to float outside and may move when the camera or observer moves. Lens flare produces dots, discs or translucent shapes related to the position of a bright light elsewhere in the frame. Autofocus, digital stabilisation, low-light noise and rolling-shutter effects can make a distant point light pulse, stretch or move erratically. A video is therefore not automatically stronger evidence than a visual report; investigators need the original file and the circumstances in which it was recorded.

This is also why single enlarged frames are weak evidence. A blurred aircraft light, planet or satellite can take on a geometric shape created by the camera’s aperture, compression or sharpening software. The most useful footage includes the horizon, fixed landmarks, several seconds before and after the event and an unedited sequence showing how the object moved relative to the scene.

## How a report is tested

A credible explanation is built through elimination and positive matching. Investigators first preserve the witness’s account, then compare it against independently recorded information. The basic questions are simple but demanding:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **When exactly did it happen?** Even an uncertainty of ten or twenty minutes can change which aircraft, satellite or astronomical object was present.
2. **Where was the witness, and which way were they looking?** “Over Vancouver” or “towards the mountains” is too broad for reliable reconstruction.
3. **How long was it visible?** Seconds, minutes and half an hour point towards very different causes.
4. **Did it move against fixed landmarks or only within a zoomed image?** This separates actual angular movement from camera shake and optical effects.
5. **Were there independent witnesses?** Reports from separated locations can allow triangulation or show that a high-altitude meteor was visible across a wide region.
6. **What records survive?** Original photographs, uncompressed video, aircraft data, satellite predictions, astronomical positions and weather observations carry more weight than later retellings.

</div>

Canada does not yet have one comprehensive public system that gathers all these elements. UAP reports may reach Transport Canada, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, the Department of National Defence, civilian organisations or social media, and agencies generally pursue them only when they relate to aviation safety, public safety or national security. Sky Canada found that this fragmented arrangement leads to inconsistent data collection, limited analysis and little feedback to witnesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canada.ca.</span></span></span>

Transport Canada can record relevant aviation occurrences in the Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System, known as CADORS, but a CADORS entry is not a finding that an extraordinary vehicle was present. It is an operational record of what was reported or affected aviation. Sky Canada recommended correlating pilot and air-traffic reports with public observations so that matching aircraft, weather and other traffic can be identified more efficiently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/preview-sky-canada-report-ocsa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.gc.ca">[science.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Insufficient scientific involvement: Weather / Celestial objects 5%</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Preview: Sky Canada Report from the Office of the Chief...15 Jan 2025 — Canadians report somewhere between 600 and 1,000 UAP sightings a...</span></span></span>

UFO*BC’s published files also show that civilian investigators do not classify every submission as mysterious: they have proposed Venus, Jupiter, aircraft, satellites, Starlink and meteors for numerous provincial sightings. At the same time, these web archives contain witness narratives of uneven detail and should not be treated as a controlled scientific dataset.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufobc.ca/Sightings/sights2001_v2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufobc.ca">[UFO*BC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufobc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sights2001 v2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 2001, Vancouver, BC: This sighting was one that I had in 2001, during the summer around July...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2001</span></span></span>

## Why unresolved does not mean extraordinary

“Unresolved” can describe two very different outcomes. In a genuinely puzzling case, reasonably good information survives but no tested explanation fits it. In a much more common type, the observation cannot be resolved because basic information is missing. Combining those categories makes the unknown portion appear more mysterious than it is.

The distinction is visible in comparative figures cited by Sky Canada. France’s official GEIPAN programme had left only 3.2 per cent of its collected cases unidentified after investigation by 2025, while 31.4 per cent could not be explained because the available information was inadequate. MUFON Canada similarly reported that 31 per cent of the cases it investigated in 2023 could not be concluded because the accounts were too vague. These figures are not specific measurements of British Columbia, but they demonstrate why “not identified” must not be read as “shown to be anomalous”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.gc.ca">[science.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s or experimental aircraft can also be classified as UAPs by observers...</span></span></span>

A report is particularly weak when it was submitted long after the event, gives only a broad date, relies on estimated size and speed, or contains no direction of view. Memory does not preserve a scene like a recording. Later discussion, media coverage and familiar UFO imagery can alter how a witness describes shape, motion or sequence without any dishonesty being involved.

Conversely, a report deserves further attention when it has an exact timestamp, multiple independent witnesses, original imagery, stable landmarks and corroborating instrumental data. Even then, “deserves investigation” is not the same as “evidence of extraterrestrial technology”. The next step is still to compare the observation with aircraft, astronomical, satellite, meteorological and imaging data.

For British Columbia, the broad pattern is therefore less dramatic but more informative than claims of a provincial mystery zone. Ordinary objects generate extraordinary-looking experiences when viewed across water, between mountain ridges, through coastal weather or without a dependable sense of range. A smaller residue remains open chiefly because Canada’s evidence collection has been inconsistent. The most responsible conclusion is not that every report has been solved, but that the word “unidentified” usually marks the beginning of an investigation—or the limits of the surviving information—rather than the discovery of an extraordinary craft.
