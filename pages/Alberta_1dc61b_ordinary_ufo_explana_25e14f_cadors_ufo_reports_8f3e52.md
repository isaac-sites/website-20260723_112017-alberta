## Introduction

A CADORS entry describing a UFO in [Alberta]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-albertas-ufo/' | relative_url }}) is often misunderstood. It does **not** mean that Transport Canada has concluded an extraordinary aircraft was present, nor does it represent the outcome of a completed investigation. Instead, a Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS) record is an operational aviation occurrence created so that potentially relevant safety information can be shared quickly with aviation authorities and industry. If a pilot reports an unidentified light, object or possible hazard, that report may appear in CADORS before anyone knows exactly what was seen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/aviation-publications/civil-aviation-daily-occurrence-reporting-system-cadors-manual-tp-4044" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport CanadaCivil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS) Manual - TP 4044September 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 5, 2025</span></span></span>

For readers interested in Alberta's UFO history, this distinction is crucial. CADORS is valuable because it preserves contemporaneous reports from pilots, air traffic controllers and other aviation personnel. However, the database records the existence of an aviation concern rather than proving that an unexplained craft was present.

## Why pilots report unidentified lights

Commercial and private pilots are trained to report anything that could affect flight safety, even when they cannot identify it immediately. An object does not need to be mysterious to justify a report; it only needs to be potentially relevant to aviation.

In Alberta, reports may originate from busy airspace around Calgary and Edmonton, military activity, regional airports, or aircraft crossing the province at high altitude. Flight crews have only seconds to assess unfamiliar lights while continuing to fly safely. Their first responsibility is hazard reporting, not solving the mystery.

## Why Alberta CADORS entries can be misleading

Because CADORS records are public and searchable, UFO researchers frequently discover Alberta entries containing terms such as "UFO", "unidentified lights" or similar descriptions. These are sometimes presented online as official confirmation of unexplained craft.

That interpretation goes further than the evidence allows.

A CADORS record usually tells readers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* that somebody reported something unusual;
* where and approximately when it occurred;
* what the initial observer described;
* why aviation authorities considered the report worth recording.

</div>

It generally does **not** establish:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* what the object ultimately proved to be;
* whether multiple witnesses independently confirmed it;
* whether radar supported the observation;
* whether photographs or sensor data existed;
* whether investigators reached any extraordinary conclusion.

</div>

The wording often reflects the uncertainty present during the event itself rather than uncertainty that remained after later analysis.

## What evidence would strengthen a case

Some CADORS reports attract continuing attention because they involve experienced flight crews. Pilot testimony deserves to be taken seriously, but aviation experience alone cannot determine the nature of an observed object.

A stronger case would normally include several independent forms of evidence, such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* multiple aircraft observing the same object from different positions;
* corroboration from air traffic controllers;
* radar or other sensor confirmation;
* precise timing allowing astronomical and satellite checks;
* photographs or video with reliable metadata;
* weather information consistent with the observation;
* evidence excluding nearby aircraft, drones or balloons.

</div>

When several independent sources agree, confidence increases that the reported event occurred substantially as described. That still does not establish an extraordinary explanation, but it provides investigators with a firmer evidential foundation.

## What evidence would weaken a case

Many Alberta CADORS entries remain difficult to interpret simply because the available information is limited.

A report becomes less persuasive when:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* only one observer saw the object;
* no radar or electronic detection exists;
* the viewing time was very brief;
* the description changed significantly afterwards;
* later checks identify known aircraft, satellites or astronomical objects;
* weather conditions could plausibly account for unusual visual effects.

</div>

The absence of follow-up should not automatically be interpreted as suppression or confirmation. In many instances there is simply insufficient evidence to move beyond the initial operational report.

## How the Sky Canada Project places CADORS in context

The Office of the Chief Science Advisor's Sky Canada Project reviewed how unidentified aerial phenomena are reported across Canada and described CADORS as one element within a broader reporting system.

The review explains that pilots generally report sightings through air traffic services, after which Transport Canada may create a CADORS record. Depending on the available information, analysts assign occurrence categories that include "Weather Balloon, Meteor, Rocket, UFO and Intelligence Sighting", laser interference or other operational events. The report also emphasises that CADORS was created for aviation occurrence management rather than as a dedicated scientific investigation programme for unexplained aerial phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canada.ca.</span></span></span>

This distinction helps explain why Alberta contains genuine pilot reports without implying that Transport Canada has verified extraordinary objects.

## Reading Alberta CADORS reports responsibly

For anyone researching Alberta's UFO history, CADORS is an important historical resource because it preserves reports that might otherwise have disappeared from the public record. It documents what aviation personnel reported at the time and shows that unidentified aerial observations are treated as legitimate aviation safety concerns.

The most reliable approach is to view each Alberta CADORS entry as the **starting point** for investigation rather than the conclusion. A report gains credibility when supported by independent witnesses, technical evidence and later analysis. It becomes less persuasive when it remains only a brief preliminary occurrence with no corroborating information.

That is why a CADORS entry labelled "UFO" should be understood as evidence that an unidentified object was **reported**, not evidence that an extraordinary craft was **proved** to exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/aviation-publications/civil-aviation-daily-occurrence-reporting-system-cadors-manual-tp-4044" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport CanadaCivil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS) Manual - TP 4044September 5, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 5, 2025</span></span></span>
