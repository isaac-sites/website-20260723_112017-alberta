## Introduction

Fredericton became an important centre of Canadian UFO history not because a famous sighting occurred there, but because physicist and UFO researcher Stanton T. Friedman lived and worked in the city for decades, building an unusually large private research collection. After his retirement, much of that material entered the Provincial Archives of [New Brunswick]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-new-brunswicks/' | relative_url }}), where archivists began the long task of arranging, describing and preserving it. The Fredericton Region Museum also developed an exhibition using records and memorabilia associated with his career.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://activehistory.ca/blog/2020/05/12/archivists-in-isolation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: activehistory.ca">[activehistory.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">activehistory.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Active History Archivists in Isolation – Active History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Active History Archivists in Isolation – Active History</span></span></span>

That legacy matters even to readers who reject Friedman’s belief that some UFOs were extraterrestrial craft. His papers preserve correspondence, [witness accounts]({{ 'witness-accounts-138882/' | relative_url }}), government-document requests, lecture records, photographs, recordings and internal debates from more than half a century of UFO culture. They can help historians examine how cases were investigated, promoted, challenged and remembered. What they cannot do by themselves is prove that Friedman’s conclusions were correct.

## Why did Friedman make Fredericton his base?

Friedman was born in the United States and trained at the University of Chicago, receiving undergraduate and master’s degrees in physics. He later worked on nuclear and aerospace projects before leaving full-time industrial physics in 1970 to concentrate on UFO research and public lecturing. He eventually moved to Fredericton with his wife Marilyn, a New Brunswick native, around the beginning of the 1980s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mag.uchicago.edu/science-medicine/science-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mag.uchicago.edu">[mag.uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mag.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science? Fredericton was therefore not merely the place where his papers happened to end up after his death. It was the working base from which he corresponded with witnesses and other researchers, prepared lectures, pursued archival enquiries and commented on cases across Canada and the United States. A University of Chicago profile described the basement study of his Fredericton home as crowded with open filing cabinets, books, rolled documents and loose papers. That disorder later became a serious archival problem, but it also reflected the scale and intensity of his collecting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mag.uchicago.edu/science-medicine/science-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mag.uchicago.edu">[mag.uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mag.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science? Fiction?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science? Fiction?</span></span></span>

His public persona made him unusually visible within the city. Fredericton’s mayor proclaimed 27 August 2007 Stanton Friedman Day, while local institutions later treated his career as part of the region’s cultural history. The connection gave New Brunswick a place in international UFO discussion that was disproportionate to the province’s own number of major sightings. Friedman might be speaking about Roswell, alleged government secrecy or an American witness, but he was doing much of the underlying correspondence and preparation from Fredericton.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mag.uchicago.edu/science-medicine/science-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mag.uchicago.edu">[mag.uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mag.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science? Fiction?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science? Fiction?</span></span></span>

His importance came especially from his role in reviving the Roswell story. Beginning in the late 1970s, Friedman pursued former United States Army Air Forces intelligence officer Jesse Marcel and other witnesses connected with the debris recovered near Roswell, New Mexico, in 1947. That work helped transform a largely forgotten incident into the best-known UFO controversy in North America. Later books, documentaries and investigations greatly expanded the story, but Friedman’s interviews and advocacy were central to its return to public attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mag.uchicago.edu/science-medicine/science-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mag.uchicago.edu">[mag.uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mag.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science? Fiction?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science? Fiction?</span></span></span>

Fredericton’s UFO legacy is therefore partly indirect. The city was not the site of Roswell or the Betty and Barney Hill case, but it became a production centre for research, lectures and media appearances that shaped how those events were understood.

## What is preserved in the Stanton Friedman fonds?

The Provincial Archives of New Brunswick identifies the collection as the Stanton T. Friedman fonds, covering material from approximately the 1950s to 2019. In archival terminology, a *fonds* is the body of records accumulated by a person or organisation through their activities, rather than an artificial selection of documents grouped only by subject. This distinction is important because the Friedman records can reveal not just what he collected, but how he worked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://activehistory.ca/blog/2020/05/12/archivists-in-isolation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: activehistory.ca">[Active History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">activehistory.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Active History Archivists in Isolation – Active History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Active History Archivists in Isolation – Active History</span></span></span>

The scale of the donation was exceptional. Provincial archivist Joanna Aiton Kerr described five cargo vans of records and called the collection the largest and most difficult processing challenge of her 20-year career. The papers arrived in a highly disorganised state, with conventional research material mixed alongside exhibition objects, recordings and novelty items.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://activehistory.ca/blog/2020/05/12/archivists-in-isolation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: activehistory.ca">[Active History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">activehistory.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Active History Archivists in Isolation – Active History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Active History Archivists in Isolation – Active History</span></span></span>

Her description indicates that the fonds contains:

This mixture makes the archive broader than a conventional file of sighting reports. It documents witness testimony, but also the social networks, commercial media, lecture circuit and argumentative culture surrounding modern UFO research. Letters between investigators may show how claims changed during circulation. Draft manuscripts can preserve discarded arguments. Travel records can establish when Friedman met particular witnesses, while government-document requests can show what records were available to him at different stages of a controversy.

The collection also includes substantial audiovisual material. A 2022 Provincial Archives newsletter reported that 600 audio records from the fonds had been digitised, following earlier processing work on visual and audiovisual records. Digitisation protects fragile originals and may eventually make selected material easier to consult, but it should not be confused with complete online access to the whole collection. A fonds of this size must be assessed, arranged and described before researchers can use it efficiently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.gnb.ca/Associates/Newsletters/2022-54-fall-e.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gnb.ca">[archives.gnb.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gnb.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Autumn 2022 Number 54November 23, 2022 — MC4244 Stanton Friedman fonds. 600 audio records have been digitized. The Fredericton Region Museum holds or displays a related body of artefacts, records and memorabilia provided by Friedman’s family. Its exhibition, *Stanton Friedman Is Out of This World*, opened in 2021 and included archival documents as well as material connected with his lectures and investigations. Later museum programming stated that the exhibition contained previously unseen documents from the larger collection donated to the Provincial Archives in 2018–19.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frederictonregionmuseum.com/stanton-friedman-ufo-exhibit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frederictonregionmuseum.com">[Fredericton Region Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frederictonregionmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fredericton Region Museum Stanton Friedman Is Out Of This World Exhibit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fredericton Region MuseumStanton Friedman Is Out Of This World Exhibit - Fredericton Region Museum...</span></span></span>

The provincial archive and museum serve different purposes. The archive is intended to preserve the evidential and administrative relationships among records, while the museum interprets selected objects for a general audience. The exhibition can explain Friedman’s public career; the fonds has greater potential for detailed historical research.

## What can the papers add to UFO history?

The archive’s strongest contribution is not likely to be a single hidden document that settles whether extraterrestrial craft have visited Earth. Its greater value lies in allowing researchers to reconstruct how UFO knowledge was created.

Friedman relied heavily on witness interviews, personal correspondence and searches through declassified records. His files may therefore permit comparison between a witness’s earliest statement and later published versions. Researchers can ask whether details became more elaborate, whether interviewers suggested interpretations, whether contradictory testimony was retained, and how uncertainty was handled in books or lectures. These are crucial questions because many famous UFO cases depend on memories recorded years after the alleged event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mag.uchicago.edu/science-medicine/science-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mag.uchicago.edu">[mag.uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mag.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science? Fiction?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science? Fiction?</span></span></span>

The papers may also clarify relationships within UFO research communities. Aiton Kerr specifically noted “sparring amongst ufologists”, suggesting that the records preserve disagreements rather than a unified pro-UFO position. Such correspondence can reveal disputes over witnesses, documents, money, publication credit and standards of proof. This is valuable because public accounts often reduce UFO controversies to a simple division between believers and sceptics, when researchers who accepted extraordinary possibilities frequently disagreed sharply with one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://activehistory.ca/blog/2020/05/12/archivists-in-isolation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: activehistory.ca">[Active History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">activehistory.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Active History Archivists in Isolation – Active History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Active History Archivists in Isolation – Active History</span></span></span>

Friedman’s worldwide archival requests are another important element. They can show which government files he consulted, what he could not obtain, and how he interpreted gaps in the record. A missing document may have been taken by Friedman as evidence of secrecy, while an archivist or historian might regard the same absence as normal destruction, incomplete cataloguing or a mistaken reference. Preserving the requests alongside replies allows those competing readings to be tested.

The archive can also place UFO history within the development of twentieth-century media. Friedman delivered his “Flying Saucers ARE Real” lecture internationally, appeared on television and radio, and adapted complex claims into repeatable arguments for public audiences. His notes, slides, scripts and recordings can show which evidence he regarded as persuasive, how his presentation changed over time and how scientific credentials were used to establish authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mag.uchicago.edu/science-medicine/science-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mag.uchicago.edu">[mag.uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mag.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science? Fiction?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science? Fiction?</span></span></span>

For New Brunswick, this creates a distinct research resource. Library and Archives Canada preserves federal reports generated by agencies such as the Department of National Defence, National Research Council, Department of Transport and Royal Canadian Mounted Police. The Friedman fonds records the parallel civilian world: the researcher receiving tips, filing requests, interviewing witnesses, arguing with critics and turning records into public narratives. The two kinds of archive answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Does preservation strengthen Friedman’s claims?

Archival preservation strengthens the historical record of Friedman’s work. It does not automatically strengthen the factual case for alien visitation.

A document in an archive is authentic as an archival object when its origin and custody can be established: it may genuinely be a letter received by Friedman, a draft he wrote or a recording he collected. That does not make every statement inside it accurate. A sincere witness can misidentify Venus, aircraft lights or a satellite. A government memo can repeat an unverified report. A researcher’s annotation can express a conviction rather than a demonstrated conclusion.

This distinction is especially important for the alleged Majestic 12 documents, which describe a secret American group supposedly formed to manage extraterrestrial technology. Friedman defended some of the material and devoted considerable effort to typography, dates and archival context, although he also identified certain related papers as false. United States archival and law-enforcement assessments have treated the central Majestic 12 material as bogus or unsupported, and it remains widely regarded by historians and sceptical investigators as a collection of forgeries or planted documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanton_T._Friedman" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stanton T. Friedman</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stanton T. Friedman</span></span></span>

Roswell presents a similar problem. Friedman’s interviews helped establish the modern crash-retrieval narrative, making his original notes historically significant. Yet later United States Air Force investigations attributed the 1947 debris to Project Mogul, a classified balloon programme designed to detect Soviet nuclear tests. Supporters dispute that explanation, but preservation of Friedman’s files cannot by itself decide between his interpretation and the official one. It instead gives researchers a better opportunity to compare sources, dates and changes in testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/3916193/roswell-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[TIME]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">roswell history</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brazel encontró restos extraños en su rancho cerca de Roswell, N.M. Tras contactar al Sheriff George Wilcox, quien a su vez llamó la base...</span></span></span>

The same caution applies to physical items sent to him. An unidentified substance in a labelled vial may be interesting evidence about what a correspondent believed or wanted investigated. Until it has a secure chain of custody, documented origin and reliable laboratory testing, it is not strong evidence of an extraordinary event.

Friedman’s scientific training should also be represented accurately. He was a qualified physicist who had worked on nuclear and aerospace projects, and he brought persistence, technical literacy and archival curiosity to UFO research. However, expertise in nuclear physics does not make every historical inference, witness assessment or document-authentication judgement correct. His credentials deserve recognition without turning them into a substitute for independent verification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mag.uchicago.edu/science-medicine/science-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mag.uchicago.edu">[mag.uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mag.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science? ## How archives can change later interpretations

An organised archive can either strengthen or weaken a famous story. It may uncover an early statement that matches a witness’s later account, supporting continuity. It may instead reveal that a dramatic detail appeared only after repeated interviews, media attention or contact with other researchers. Both outcomes are useful.

Friedman’s papers are particularly suited to this kind of work because he researched long-running controversies in which testimony evolved over decades. A historian could compare private correspondence, recorded interviews, lecture scripts and published books to identify when particular claims entered the narrative. That method is more reliable than treating the latest and most polished version of a story as though it were the original account.

Archival context can also expose selection effects. Friedman publicly presented cases he considered strongest, but the complete files may include abandoned leads, weak reports, failed attempts to confirm witnesses and objections that did not appear in final publications. Examining those materials would help determine whether his conclusions emerged from systematic comparison or from concentrating on evidence that favoured his existing position.

Conversely, the archive may preserve information overlooked by critics: original recordings, contemporaneous notes or documentary trails showing that an interview took place earlier than previously believed. Proper archival work does not guarantee a sceptical or supportive result. Its purpose is to make the history more traceable.

The surviving records can also document how terminology changed. Friedman preferred the phrase “flying saucer” and argued that only a small fraction of unidentified reports represented genuinely extraordinary craft. Modern institutions increasingly use “unidentified anomalous phenomena”, or UAP, but the evidential problem remains much the same: an unexplained observation is not automatically an alien vehicle. Better records can narrow the possibilities, though they cannot compensate for missing measurements or unreliable testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mag.uchicago.edu/science-medicine/science-fiction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mag.uchicago.edu">[mag.uchicago.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mag.uchicago.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science? ## Fredericton’s lasting place in Canadian UFO culture

Fredericton’s importance rests on three connected developments: Friedman chose the city as his home and working base; provincial archivists accepted the records accumulated during his career; and a local museum converted part of that legacy into a public exhibition. Together, they made the city both a repository for specialist research and a place where UFO history entered New Brunswick’s wider cultural heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://activehistory.ca/blog/2020/05/12/archivists-in-isolation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: activehistory.ca">[activehistory.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">activehistory.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Active History Archivists in Isolation – Active History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Active History Archivists in Isolation – Active History</span></span></span>

This does not make Fredericton Canada’s official centre for UFO investigation. The Provincial Archives of New Brunswick is preserving a private researcher’s fonds, not endorsing his conclusions or operating a scientific UAP programme. Nor should the collection be confused with federal Canadian UFO files held by Library and Archives Canada.

Its real significance is more durable. Many private UFO collections disappear when a researcher dies, leaving later writers dependent on published accounts stripped of correspondence, failed leads and working notes. Friedman arranged for a large part of his intellectual and professional record to survive within a public institution. That creates the possibility of more careful scholarship than either celebratory retellings or easy ridicule.

Fredericton’s Stanton Friedman legacy is therefore best understood as an archival achievement rather than proof of an extraordinary claim. The preserved papers may illuminate how some of North America’s most influential UFO narratives were assembled, debated and sold to the public. Whether future research vindicates particular details or exposes further weaknesses, the archive ensures that those judgements can be based on a richer record than the legend alone.
