## Introduction

The reported radioactivity associated with the 1967 Falcon Lake incident is often presented as one of its strongest pieces of [physical evidence]({{ 'physical-evidence/' | relative_url }}). In reality, it illustrates the opposite problem. Some laboratory tests did detect radioactive material in samples linked to the case, but investigators were never able to establish an unbroken chain of custody showing exactly where those samples came from, who handled them at every stage, or whether they accurately represented the alleged [landing site]({{ 'landing-site/' | relative_url }}). That broken evidence chain greatly limits what the laboratory results can prove. Within Manitoba's UFO history, the radioactivity remains an intriguing detail, but not reliable evidence that an unidentified craft landed at Falcon Lake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/engage-learn/podcasts/discover/episode-054.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFOs at LAC: The Falcon Lake incident, part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at LAC: The Falcon Lake incident, part 2 - Canada.caMay 29, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2019</span></span></span>

## How the samples were collected

The central weakness lies not in the laboratory measurements themselves but in how the evidence reached the laboratory.

Stefan Michalak initially told investigators that he had left the site undisturbed. However, before officials conducted a fully documented examination, he returned to the location on his own and collected items including soil, a burned shirt and a steel measuring tape. These materials were later submitted for testing after having already passed through private hands. Because the collection was unsupervised, investigators could not verify the precise locations from which individual samples originated or exclude the possibility of accidental contamination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

The timing also mattered. By the time the RCMP, Royal Canadian Air Force personnel and other investigators carried out their documented inspection, the scene had already been disturbed. Whatever physical traces might once have existed could no longer be separated confidently from changes introduced by weather, visitors or Michalak's own activities. Library and Archives Canada has highlighted this sequence as one of the major unresolved weaknesses in the case, noting that investigators were left asking exactly where individual samples had originated but could no longer answer that question with confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/engage-learn/podcasts/discover/episode-054.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFOs at LAC: The Falcon Lake incident, part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at LAC: The Falcon Lake incident, part 2 - Canada.caMay 29, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2019</span></span></span>

This is a classic example of why forensic investigations place such importance on chain of custody. Even genuine laboratory findings become much less persuasive when the origin of the tested material cannot be independently verified.

## What the laboratory readings could establish

Some of the submitted materials did produce elevated radiation readings. Government laboratories examined soil, the burned shirt and the steel tape for radioactive contamination, while later testing also examined metallic fragments reportedly recovered from rock fissures near the claimed landing area. Official memoranda recorded measurable radioactivity in some submitted items, prompting authorities to consider whether any public health risk existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.experiencersupport.org/declassified/safety-assessment-regarding-radiation-hazards-at-falcon-beach-manitoba-september" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: experiencersupport.org">[experiencersupport.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">experiencersupport.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Safety Assessment: Regarding Radiation Hazards at Falcon Beach, Manitoba – September 13 1967 &#124; The Experiencer Support AssociationSeptemb...</span></span></span>

Those laboratory results establish only a limited set of facts:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Some submitted materials contained radioactive substances.
* The radioactivity was measurable rather than imaginary.
* Government agencies considered the findings sufficiently unusual to investigate further.

</div>

They do **not** establish that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the radioactivity originated from an unidentified craft;
* the material was deposited during the reported encounter;
* every tested sample genuinely came from the claimed landing location; or
* the radiation represented an unknown technology.

</div>

When investigators later surveyed the alleged landing site with radiation detection equipment, they did not discover widespread dangerous contamination. Instead, they detected only small areas of radioactivity that investigators considered consistent with ordinary terrestrial sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

## Natural minerals, contamination and possible planting

Several ordinary explanations remain consistent with the available evidence.

The [Falcon Lake]({{ 'falcon-lake/' | relative_url }}) area lies within the Precambrian Shield, a region containing naturally occurring uranium-bearing minerals. Radioactive minerals therefore are not unexpected in parts of the local geology. Government investigators and later commentators noted that naturally occurring radioactive material could explain at least some of the measurements obtained from the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

Laboratory analysis also pointed towards another possibility. The low-level radioactivity resembled contamination from radium-based luminous paint—the type once widely used on instrument dials, watches and other manufactured products. Contemporary investigators specifically considered this a plausible explanation for some of the detected radioactivity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

The metallic fragments found later illustrate the same evidential problem. Analysis indicated that they consisted largely of relatively pure silver coated with a sticky substance to which radioactive uranium-bearing material had adhered. That makes the fragments unusual, but it does not demonstrate an extraterrestrial origin. Because they were reportedly recovered long after the original investigation and without continuous forensic control, critics have argued that accidental contamination, geological contact or even deliberate planting cannot be ruled out. Even Chris Rutkowski, discussing the evidence for Library and Archives Canada, emphasised that the samples' provenance had been compromised before independent examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/engage-learn/podcasts/discover/episode-054.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFOs at LAC: The Falcon Lake incident, part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at LAC: The Falcon Lake incident, part 2 - Canada.caMay 29, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2019</span></span></span>

## Why the evidence chain matters more than the radiation

The Falcon Lake case demonstrates an important principle in evaluating physical evidence. Scientific instruments can accurately measure radioactivity, but they cannot reconstruct an uncertain history of how a sample was collected.

Had investigators reached an untouched landing site immediately, documented the exact locations of radioactive materials, photographed them in place and maintained continuous custody from collection to laboratory analysis, the findings would carry substantially greater evidential weight. Instead, the sequence was interrupted by unsupervised collection, delayed official examination and incomplete documentation of sample origins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/engage-learn/podcasts/discover/episode-054.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFOs at LAC: The Falcon Lake incident, part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at LAC: The Falcon Lake incident, part 2 - Canada.caMay 29, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2019</span></span></span>

For that reason, historians, forensic investigators and sceptics generally distinguish between **detecting radioactivity** and **proving a UFO landing**. The former occurred. The latter did not.

Within the broader debate over witness credibility in [Manitoba]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-manitobas-ufo/' | relative_url }})'s UFO history, the radioactive samples therefore occupy an unusual position. They prevent the Falcon Lake case from being dismissed as a story with no physical evidence at all, yet the broken chain of custody prevents them from serving as convincing proof that an unidentified craft was ever present. That balance explains why the radioactivity remains one of the most discussed—and least conclusive—elements of Canada's best-known UFO incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/engage-learn/podcasts/discover/episode-054.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFOs at LAC: The Falcon Lake incident, part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at LAC: The Falcon Lake incident, part 2 - Canada.caMay 29, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2019</span></span></span>
