## Introduction

The available evidence does **not** show that [Saskatchewan]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-saskatchewans/' | relative_url }}) experienced clearly documented province-wide UFO sighting waves that can be reconstructed from existing records. The main obstacle is not simply the number of reports but the way they were collected. Canadian UFO reports have historically been divided across federal departments, aviation safety systems, police files, civilian researchers, local media and private databases that were never designed to operate as a single reporting network. As a result, apparent rises or gaps in Saskatchewan [sightings]({{ 'sightings/' | relative_url }}) may reflect changes in reporting practices as much as changes in what people actually saw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

This fragmented reporting landscape matters because readers naturally ask whether years with more Saskatchewan reports represent genuine waves of unusual aerial activity or merely better documentation. The surviving evidence suggests caution. Some individual cases are well preserved, while many others appear only in one archive or database, making province-wide reconstruction incomplete and sometimes impossible.

## What civilian databases record – and what they miss

The most comprehensive modern civilian source is the Canadian UFO Survey, which compiles reports from investigators, media coverage, websites, social media and direct submissions into a standardised database. Each case is coded for variables such as date, location, witness numbers, reliability, duration and possible explanation, allowing researchers to compare reports across provinces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canadianuforeport.ca/survey-data" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canadianuforeport.ca">[Canadian UFO Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canadianuforeport.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canadian UFO Report Canadian UFO Survey Data</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canadian UFO Report Canadian UFO Survey Data</span></span></span>

That standardisation is valuable, but it has important limits when trying to identify Saskatchewan sighting waves.

Civilian databases generally depend on reports reaching participating investigators or becoming publicly visible. A sighting that was reported only to police, an airport, an air traffic authority or a government office may never appear in a civilian catalogue. Conversely, a dramatic case that received extensive media attention may generate far more documentation than an equally unusual but unpublicised event.

Several additional factors complicate interpretation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* older reports were often entered retrospectively rather than at the time of the sighting;
* reporting organisations have changed over the decades;
* internet reporting greatly increased opportunities for witnesses to submit cases;
* some incidents appear in multiple sources while others survive in only one;
* many records contain incomplete information, limiting statistical comparison.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canadianuforeport.ca/survey-data" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canadianuforeport.ca">[Canadian UFO Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canadianuforeport.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canadian UFO Report Canadian UFO Survey Data</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canadian UFO Report Canadian UFO Survey Data</span></span></span>

</div>

For Saskatchewan, where annual totals are generally modest compared with Canada's most populous provinces, even a handful of missing or duplicated reports can noticeably alter the apparent pattern of activity.

## How federal and aviation systems treat sightings differently

Canada has never operated one continuous national UFO reporting programme. Instead, responsibility has shifted repeatedly between departments and agencies.

During the early Cold War, projects such as Project Second Storey attempted to coordinate government interest in unidentified aerial reports, but proposed standard reporting procedures were never widely adopted. Later, reports reached organisations including the Department of National Defence, Department of Transport, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police and eventually the National Research Council (NRC), which collected public reports until 1995 before transferring its files to Library and Archives Canada.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

Each organisation collected information for different reasons.

* **Transport and [aviation]({{ 'aviation-fa870c/' | relative_url }}) authorities** primarily focused on flight safety and operational hazards.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The RCMP** recorded incidents brought to police attention rather than actively searching for UFO events.
* **The Department of National Defence** concentrated on defence and security implications rather than unexplained observations in general.
* **The NRC** often acted as a scientific clearing house, attempting conventional explanations for reports submitted by the public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

</div>

Because these systems had different objectives, identical Saskatchewan sightings could receive very different treatment depending on where they were reported. A pilot's observation might enter an aviation record, while a nearby civilian witness might submit the same event to a local investigator, with no formal mechanism linking the two records.

## Why archival gaps obstruct province-wide reconstruction

Library and Archives Canada now preserves thousands of historical Canadian UFO-related documents from several federal organisations, providing an invaluable resource for researchers. However, the archive itself illustrates why reconstructing provincial waves remains difficult.

The collection spans many decades and includes correspondence, reporting forms, memoranda and investigation files. Yet not every document identifies a precise location, many files concern administrative procedures rather than sightings, and some historical reporting streams were never comprehensive in the first place. Roughly half of the digitised records identify a specific sighting location, while many others do not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

For Saskatchewan this creates several practical problems:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* there is no guarantee every provincial report reached federal authorities;
* local newspaper accounts may describe incidents absent from government files;
* government files may preserve reports that never entered civilian databases;
* matching records across separate archives often requires manual comparison because no unified national identifier exists.

</div>

These gaps make it risky to interpret missing reports as evidence that nothing unusual occurred.

## Why apparent waves may reflect reporting behaviour

When researchers observe increases in Saskatchewan reports during particular years, they must distinguish between a **reporting wave** and a **sighting wave**.

Reporting behaviour can change because of:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* increased publicity surrounding UFO stories;
* easier online reporting systems;
* changing participation by civilian investigators;
* renewed public interest following international developments;
* improved preservation of reports that might previously have been lost.

</div>

The recent Sky Canada Project reached similar conclusions about Canada's reporting landscape, describing a fragmented system in which multiple organisations receive information using different procedures and recommending greater coordination rather than assuming existing datasets represent a complete national record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

This does not mean Saskatchewan's reported increases are meaningless. They may indicate periods of greater observation, public concern or local attention. However, they cannot by themselves demonstrate a province-wide wave comparable to a tightly documented sequence of interconnected sightings.

## What would strengthen evidence for a Saskatchewan wave?

A convincing reconstruction would require independent reporting systems to converge on the same pattern.

The strongest evidence would include multiple features occurring together:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* civilian reports showing similar objects over a short period;
* independent aviation observations from pilots or air traffic personnel;
* corresponding federal records documenting the same dates and locations;
* police or emergency service reports that can be matched chronologically;
* sufficient witness detail to evaluate conventional explanations.

</div>

Such convergence is uncommon in the surviving Saskatchewan record. Most documented cases stand largely on their own rather than forming a continuous chain across several reporting systems.

## The most reliable conclusion

The fragmented nature of Canadian reporting systems makes it difficult to determine whether Saskatchewan ever experienced a true province-wide UFO sighting wave. Civilian surveys, aviation reporting, federal archives and historical police records each preserve genuine pieces of the historical record, but none captures the whole picture.

The result is a patchwork rather than a continuous timeline. Individual Saskatchewan incidents can often be investigated in considerable detail, but statistical rises in annual reports should be interpreted carefully. Until records from separate reporting systems can be consistently linked, increases in reported sightings remain evidence of changing documentation as much as possible changes in unexplained aerial phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>
