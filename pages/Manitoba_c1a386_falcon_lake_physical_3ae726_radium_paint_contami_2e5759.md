## Introduction

One of the most important ordinary explanations examined during the investigation of the 1967 [Falcon Lake]({{ 'falcon-lake/' | relative_url }}) incident was that the radioactive material associated with Stefan Michalak's samples did not originate from an unknown craft at all. Instead, officials considered whether the detected radium-226 could have come from contamination by commercially available luminous paint. This mattered because the presence of measurable radioactivity was one of the case's strongest pieces of [physical evidence]({{ 'physical-evidence/' | relative_url }}). If an everyday source could account for those readings, the radioactive findings would no longer support an extraordinary explanation.

The radium-paint hypothesis was never proved, but neither was it eliminated. ## Why radium-226 drew official attention

Laboratory testing of material submitted by Michalak—including soil, a burnt shirt and a steel measuring tape—identified gamma radiation consistent with radium-226 or a similar source. Initial measurements were high enough that Canadian officials briefly considered whether the material represented a genuine public health concern before carrying out more detailed field surveys.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

Radium-226 immediately attracted attention because it was not an exotic substance. By the 1960s it was well known to scientists and radiation specialists, having been widely used for decades in self-luminous paints applied to:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* aircraft instruments;
* military compasses;
* clocks and watches;
* surveying equipment; and
* other dials intended to glow in the dark.

</div>

Although the visible glow fades over time, the radium itself remains radioactive for thousands of years. Old flakes or dust from such paint can therefore still produce detectable radiation long after the object appears ordinary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nuclearsafety.gc.ca/eng/resources/radiation/could-your-collectible-item-contain-radium.cfm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuclearsafety.gc.ca">[nuclearsafety.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuclearsafety.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Could your collectible item contain radium?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 27, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 27, 2024</span></span></span>

For investigators, this meant that detecting radium did not automatically imply exposure to an unusual energy source. It could equally indicate contamination from a familiar industrial product.

## How luminous paint could contaminate samples

The contamination theory did not require a large quantity of radioactive material. Instead, investigators considered whether a small amount of radium-bearing paint or dust might have adhered to the objects submitted for testing.

Several mechanisms were plausible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Direct contact.** A measuring tape, clothing or collected rock fragments could have touched an old luminous instrument or loose radium paint.
* **Transfer during handling.** If contaminated material came into contact with samples after they were collected, radioactive particles could have been transferred without changing the appearance of the objects.
* **Environmental contamination.** Small fragments of radium paint discarded from industrial or military equipment could potentially contaminate isolated locations.

</div>

This possibility became especially relevant because many of the radioactive items reached laboratories only after being collected, transported and handled by Michalak himself before entering official custody. That sequence made it difficult to prove exactly when any contamination occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

Importantly, the radium-paint explanation addressed the radioactive readings themselves rather than Michalak's reported [burns]({{ 'burns/' | relative_url }}). It was a proposed mechanism for the physical evidence, not a complete explanation for every aspect of the incident.

## Why no ordinary source was conclusively proved

Although chemists regarded luminous radium paint as a credible explanation, investigators never identified a specific contaminated object that could be tied directly to Michalak or to the Falcon Lake site.

Several uncertainties prevented a definitive conclusion.

First, the radioactive samples had an imperfect chain of custody. The most significant material was collected weeks after the original event, after the site had already been revisited and after Michalak had handled the objects himself. That reduced confidence in determining exactly where any contamination originated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

Second, when officials conducted systematic surveys of the location, they did not find widespread dangerous contamination. Instead, they detected only limited radioactive traces in a small area rather than evidence of a large radioactive source. This finding was compatible with localised contamination but did not identify its cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

Third, radium-226 also occurs naturally as part of the uranium decay chain. Because parts of the Canadian Shield, including areas around Whiteshell Provincial Park, contain naturally occurring radioactive minerals, investigators also had to consider whether some readings reflected local geology rather than artificial contamination. The available evidence did not clearly distinguish between these possibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nuclearsafety.gc.ca/eng/resources/radiation/could-your-collectible-item-contain-radium.cfm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuclearsafety.gc.ca">[nuclearsafety.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuclearsafety.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Could your collectible item contain radium?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 27, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 27, 2024</span></span></span>

## What the radium theory means for the Falcon Lake case

The radium-paint hypothesis illustrates an important feature of the Falcon Lake investigation: officials did not treat the discovery of radioactive material as self-evident proof of an extraordinary event. Instead, they actively searched for conventional explanations grounded in known radioactive substances.

The theory has strengths. It fits the identification of radium-226, relies on a material widely available in the mid-twentieth century, and explains how measurable radioactivity could exist without requiring an unknown propulsion system. It also aligns with the cautious approach taken by Canadian investigators, who explored industrial and environmental sources before drawing conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

At the same time, it has clear limits. No documented source of luminous paint was ever recovered, no investigator demonstrated precisely how contamination occurred, and no experiment recreated the pattern of findings from a known radium-painted object. As a result, the theory remains a plausible contamination mechanism rather than an established solution.

Within [Manitoba]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-manitobas-ufo/' | relative_url }})'s UFO history, this makes the radium-paint explanation significant not because it definitively debunks the Falcon Lake incident, but because it shows that one of the case's most frequently cited pieces of physical evidence was open to an ordinary interpretation that investigators considered seriously and were never able either to prove or to eliminate.
