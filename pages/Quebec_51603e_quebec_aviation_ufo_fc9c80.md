## Introduction

[Quebec]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-quebecs-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s busiest skies create ideal conditions for ordinary aircraft to look extraordinary. Around Montreal, passenger jets, cargo aircraft, helicopters and training flights move through overlapping airport zones, often at night and through haze, cloud or light pollution. Seen head-on, a distant aircraft may appear almost stationary; when it turns, its bright landing lights can fade abruptly and reveal a changing pattern of red, green and white lights. Without reliable distance cues, that routine manoeuvre may be remembered as hovering, sudden acceleration or disappearance.

## Montreal’s crowded aviation landscape

Greater Montreal is not served by a single isolated airport. Montréal–Trudeau handles the region’s main scheduled passenger traffic, while Montréal–Mirabel is an important cargo and aerospace centre. Montréal Metropolitan Airport at Saint-Hubert adds commercial, general-aviation and training activity on the South Shore. Transport Canada identifies Montréal–Trudeau, Mirabel and Québec City Jean Lesage as Quebec’s three airports in the National Airports System, while current airspace arrangements also place Trudeau and Saint-Hubert control zones close enough that NAV CANADA has adjusted their shared boundary to reduce controller workload.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/corporate-services/transparency/briefing-documents-transport-canada/2025-3/corporate-structure/tc-regions/quebec-region" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In 2024, the Canada Infrastructure Bank announced a $90-million investment towards construction of a new domestic commercial passenger te...</span></span></span>

This matters to observers because the visible sky does not correspond neatly to municipal boundaries. A person who says they are “nowhere near an airport” may still be beneath an arrival route, departure track, helicopter transit or holding pattern. Aircraft approaching Trudeau can remain aligned towards an observer for several minutes. Traffic associated with Mirabel may be visible far from the airport because cargo jets begin descending or turning over a broad area. Saint-Hubert adds smaller aircraft whose lower altitude, slower speed and frequent turns may look less familiar than airline traffic.

Montreal’s terminal airspace is complex enough to have its own specialised aeronautical chart. NAV CANADA describes terminal-area charts as tools for places where the airspace structure around arrivals and departures is relatively complicated. The Canada Flight Supplement likewise contains specific procedures for visual flights around Trudeau, Saint-Hubert, Mirabel and Montreal’s terminal Class C airspace. ## When aircraft lights change shape

Canadian aircraft lighting standards require combinations that are useful for collision avoidance but easily misread from the ground. A conventional aeroplane has a steady red position light on its left side, a green light on its right and white light visible towards the rear. Anti-collision systems may flash red, white or alternating colours. Landing lights are usually powerful white lamps aimed forward, so their apparent brightness depends strongly on whether the aircraft is pointing towards the observer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/corporate-services/acts-regulations/list-regulations/canadian-aviation-regulations-sor-96-433/standards/standard-625-aircraft-equipment-maintenance-standard/standard-625-schedule-1-position-anti-collision-light-systems-canadian-aviation-regulations-cars" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canada.ca.</span></span></span>

From a favourable angle, these lights make an aircraft recognisable. From an unfavourable one, they can produce several classic “UFO” effects.

**A bright light that hovers.** An approaching aircraft may travel almost directly along the observer’s line of sight. Its angular position against the sky changes very little, even though it is moving rapidly closer. The landing lights remain prominent while the body and wing lights are lost in darkness. The result can resemble a brilliant stationary object.

**A light that suddenly shoots sideways.** When the aircraft begins its base-to-final turn or follows a curved arrival route, the previously small angular movement becomes obvious. The observer may interpret the transition from head-on approach to crosswise motion as an abrupt acceleration, although the aircraft’s actual speed has changed little.

**A light that disappears instantly.** Landing lights are directional. As an aircraft turns away, the main beam can cease to point towards the witness and appear to switch off. Thin cloud, haze and patches of precipitation can sharpen the effect by alternately concealing and revealing the aircraft.

**A triangle or disc made from separate points.** Wingtip, tail, landing and anti-collision lights may be perceived as the outline of a dark structure. Several aircraft or helicopters in formation can create an even larger apparent shape. Darkness supplies no visible edges, so the mind connects the lights into a single object.

**Colour changes without a change of object.** A turn can expose different combinations of red, green and white position lights. Atmospheric scattering, windows, camera sensors and digital zoom can further alter their apparent colour. A witness may therefore describe one object changing colour when the simpler explanation is a changing viewing angle.

These effects are not merely excuses invented for UFO cases. Transport Canada itself uses aircraft light combinations to explain how observers can infer direction: seeing red and green together suggests an aircraft approaching, while red with white or green alone indicates other orientations. The same geometry that helps trained observers identify traffic can mislead people who do not know what the pattern means.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/sites/default/files/2025-03/aim-2025-1_rpa-e.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">aim 2025 1 rpa e</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">aim 2025 1 rpa e</span></span></span>

## Why distance and motion are so easy to misjudge

A point of light carries almost no built-in information about size. A small nearby drone, a helicopter several kilometres away and an airliner much farther out can occupy a similar angle in the observer’s vision. Unless the object passes in front of a known landmark, produces a clearly local sound or can be compared with radar or flight data, estimates of height, speed and dimensions are largely dependent on assumptions.

This is why apparently precise UFO descriptions can be unstable. A witness may report that a light crossed several kilometres in seconds, but that calculation usually begins by assigning it an altitude or distance that was never measured. If the assumed range is ten times too great, the calculated speed and size may also be roughly ten times too great. Reports of “impossible acceleration” are therefore weak when they are based only on movement across a featureless night sky.

One well-known visual effect is autokinesis. A small stationary light viewed against darkness can appear to drift, dart or change direction because there is no stable visual reference against which to judge it. The effect is recognised in aviation safety literature and has been demonstrated experimentally; tiny involuntary eye movements and the absence of surrounding cues contribute to the impression that the light itself is moving.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skybrary.aero/articles/autokinetic-effect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skybrary.aero">[skybrary.aero]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skybrary.aero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skybrary.aero.</span></span></span>

Cloud and haze add another layer. A bright aircraft light may illuminate moisture around it, creating a larger glowing patch than the physical source. Intervening cloud can make separate lights merge, blur or pulse. Near cities, skyglow reduces contrast and hides aircraft structure while leaving the brightest lamps visible. The witness may then see only an apparently detached luminous object.

Pilots confront related problems from the opposite viewpoint. Transport Canada warns that night flying is especially vulnerable to deteriorating weather and the “black-hole” illusion, in which an absence of ground detail distorts height and approach judgement. Aviation safety guidance also notes that unfamiliar runway environments, bright city lights, off-airport lighting and poor visibility can create false impressions of distance, alignment or altitude. These are operational hazards precisely because experience does not eliminate the limitations of human vision.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/publications/system-safety-summer-briefing-kit-tp-14112/hazards-associated-flying-night-powerpoint-presentation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada Hazards Associated with Flying at Night</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada Hazards Associated with Flying at Night</span></span></span>

## What pilots reported near Sherbrooke

One of Quebec’s more interesting recent aviation-linked reports occurred near Sherbrooke on 12 February 2023. According to reporting based on Transport Canada’s Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System, crews aboard several airliners described two or three unusual lights above the flight paths. The lights were said to move in a rapid or irregular manner, and the observations were relayed through air-traffic channels. The occurrence was later highlighted in Canadian coverage of 17 aviation UFO reports recorded during 2023.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yuwQMa0xL28" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: youtube.com">[youtube.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">youtube.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on youtube.com.</span></span></span>

The case is stronger than a lone roadside sighting in several respects. More than one flight crew appears to have noticed the lights, the event entered an official aviation reporting system, and the witnesses were accustomed to viewing aircraft and celestial objects from altitude. Multiple independent observers can reduce the risk that a report is caused solely by one person’s eyesight, imagination or camera artefact.

Yet those strengths do not answer the crucial questions. The public summary does not provide a triangulated position, measured range, confirmed altitude or instrument-derived speed. It is unclear from the published information whether every crew saw the same physical source from sufficiently separated positions to calculate its location. Descriptions such as “above the flight paths” or “moving rapidly” are impressions unless connected to angular measurements and exact aircraft positions.

Altitude can also make ordinary objects behave unexpectedly in the visual field. From a moving cockpit, distant satellites, other aircraft, stars near the horizon and lights seen through layered cloud may appear to change direction because the observing aircraft is itself turning or advancing. Reflections from cockpit windows are another possibility in some pilot cases, although an external source seen independently by several crews would make a single-aircraft reflection less convincing.

The responsible classification is therefore unresolved but data-poor, not extraordinary. The report establishes that experienced crews saw something they could not immediately identify. It does not establish a craft performing measured high-speed manoeuvres. Later publicity increased awareness of the event, but did not publicly add the sensor data needed to turn qualitative impressions into a reconstructable flight path.

## Pilot testimony is valuable, not infallible

Pilots are often presented in UFO discussions as near-perfect observers. That overstates their role. Their training gives them useful advantages: familiarity with navigation lights, weather, air routes, relative motion and standard aircraft behaviour. A pilot who reports that a light looked unlike nearby traffic deserves more weight than an unsupported social-media caption.

However, pilots are trained principally to operate aircraft safely, not to determine the distance and velocity of isolated luminous targets without instruments. Night vision, glare, fatigue, divided attention and lack of scale affect professionals as well as the public. Aviation training materials exist precisely because visual illusions can mislead experienced crews during real operations.

Credibility should also be separated from identification. A sincere and competent witness can accurately report an experience — “I saw three lights move relative to one another” — while being mistaken about its cause or distance. Calling such a person credible does not confirm their interpretation. Conversely, finding an ordinary explanation does not imply that the original witness lied.

The best pilot cases are those in which testimony is joined by information that can be checked independently: exact time, aircraft position, bearing and elevation of the light, duration, weather, radio exchanges, surveillance data and observations from more than one location. Without those elements, professional status improves the report’s seriousness but does not resolve it.

## What radar silence really means

The phrase “nothing appeared on radar” is frequently treated as decisive evidence in Quebec UFO stories, especially accounts connected with Montreal’s airports. In reality, its meaning depends on which surveillance system was consulted, what area it covered, the object’s height and size, whether it carried a functioning transponder, and whether controllers were looking at the relevant recording and time.

Modern air-traffic surveillance is layered. Secondary surveillance radar depends on signals associated with aircraft transponders, while primary radar detects reflected energy from objects within its coverage. Canada also uses technologies such as Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast, in which aircraft transmit position information. NAV CANADA documentation describes secondary-radar coverage and the continuing development of layered surveillance, including radar and ADS-B.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.navcanada.ca/en/enreng14-may-2026.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: navcanada.ca">[NAV CANADA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">navcanada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">enreng14 may 2026</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">enreng14 may 2026</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: may 2026</span></span></span>

A normal transponder-equipped airliner operating in controlled Montreal airspace should generally be conspicuous to air-traffic services. If witnesses place a large conventional aircraft at a particular altitude and position while controllers report no corresponding tracked traffic, that is relevant evidence against that simple scenario.

But radar silence cannot rule out every ordinary source:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* A light may be astronomical, atmospheric or projected on cloud and therefore have no solid radar-reflecting object at the apparent location.
* A small drone, bird, balloon or low-flying aircraft may be below useful coverage, lost in ground clutter or too weak to appear as a meaningful primary return.
* An aircraft without an operating or received transponder signal may not appear in the same way as cooperative traffic.
* The witness’s estimated position may be wrong, causing controllers or later investigators to search the wrong volume of sky.
* A report may refer only to the absence of an identified aircraft track, not to a forensic review of all raw primary-radar returns.

That may weaken an aircraft explanation, particularly for a supposedly large object in well-covered controlled airspace. It does not by itself demonstrate that a solid object was present, nor that it possessed stealth or other unusual technology.

## Approach lights and other false “objects”

Aircraft are not the only aviation lights that generate confusion. Airports contain approach-light arrays, runway edge lights, precision approach path indicators, taxiway lights and obstruction beacons. Their arrangement is designed to create recognisable geometry for pilots, but from an unfamiliar position it may resemble a luminous row, triangle or hovering cluster.

Approach and runway lighting can be seen over long distances at night. Atmospheric refraction, undulating terrain and partial obstruction by buildings or trees may reveal only part of the installation. A witness moving in a vehicle can then experience parallax: nearby objects shift rapidly against distant lights, making fixed airport lamps appear to move or separate.

Red obstruction lights on towers and tall structures are another common trap. Canadian standards include steady red lamps, flashing red beacons and white flashing systems. In mist or low cloud, only the brightest upper lights may be visible, apparently suspended without a supporting mast. Several towers viewed along the same line of sight can create an apparently organised formation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/corporate-services/acts-regulations/list-regulations/canadian-aviation-regulations-sor-96-433/standards/standard-621-obstacle-marking-lighting-canadian-aviation-regulations-cars" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada Standard 621</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada Standard 621</span></span></span>

Misidentified ground lighting becomes less likely when a light changes bearing substantially from a stationary viewpoint, passes in front of stars or cloud, or is independently tracked from separated locations. But a short video taken through a moving car window rarely supplies that information. Before treating such footage as an airborne object, investigators need the camera location, direction, movement of the observer and the positions of nearby airports, heliports and illuminated structures.

## How to assess an airport-area UFO report

The most useful investigation begins with reconstruction rather than argument. For Quebec sightings near Montreal, Québec City, Sherbrooke or regional airports, six questions usually separate a potentially interesting case from an ordinary light seen under poor conditions.

1. **Where was the witness looking?** A street address or coordinates and a compass bearing are more valuable than “over Montreal”. The same light may sit directly on an arrival path when plotted accurately.
2. **Was the observer moving?** Motion in a car, train or aircraft can create apparent movement through parallax and reflections. A stationary observation from a known location is easier to analyse.
3. **Which lights were visible?** Steady white, flashing white, red, green and alternating patterns can indicate aircraft orientation. A bright white light that later resolves into red and green is strongly consistent with an approaching aircraft turning across the line of sight.
4. **What was the exact time?** Even a difference of several minutes can match or exclude a particular flight. Approximate times sharply reduce the value of traffic reconstruction.
5. **What were the weather and cloud layers?** Haze, mist, precipitation and low cloud can enlarge, merge or intermittently hide lights. They can also conceal the aircraft body and create the impression that the glow itself is the object.
6. **What independent records exist?** Air-traffic audio, CADORS entries, airport operations, [photographs]({{ 'photographs/' | relative_url }}) from separate locations and surveillance information are more useful than repeated copies of the same video. Public flight-tracking sites can provide leads, but incomplete coverage or missing data should not be mistaken for proof that no aircraft was present.

A report becomes more persuasive when several observers at known, separated positions record the same object against background stars or landmarks, allowing triangulation. It becomes weaker when estimates of speed and size depend entirely on an assumed distance, when the only evidence is a zoomed light, or when a plausible aircraft matches the time and direction.

## The balance of evidence

Quebec’s airports and flight corridors do not explain every unusual light report. The Sherbrooke pilot observations remain publicly unresolved, and some historical cases lack enough surviving information for a confident identification. An honest assessment should preserve that uncertainty rather than assigning an explanation that the evidence cannot demonstrate.

Nevertheless, aviation provides one of the province’s most important recurring sources of false mystery. Montreal’s overlapping airport environments place many observers beneath complex traffic. Aircraft lights alter dramatically with viewing angle. Darkness removes scale and depth, autokinesis creates apparent movement, and cloud can hide airframes while magnifying lamps. Even pilots can report something genuinely unidentified when the available information is insufficient for recognition.

The key distinction is between **unidentified at the time** and **shown to be anomalous**. An airport controller, pilot or police officer may be unable to name a light during an event, while later reconstruction identifies aircraft, helicopters or fixed installations. In other cases, records remain too sparse to decide. Radar silence can narrow the options but cannot transform an ambiguous light into proof of a solid craft. Within Quebec’s UFO history, aviation-linked sightings are most valuable when they expose exactly where eyewitness evidence is strong, where it is fragile and which missing measurements would make the difference.
