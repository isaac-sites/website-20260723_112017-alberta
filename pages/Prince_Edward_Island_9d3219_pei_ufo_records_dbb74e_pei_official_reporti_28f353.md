## Introduction

[Prince Edward Island]({{ 'prince-edward-island/' | relative_url }}) has never had a single government office responsible for collecting every UFO or unidentified aerial phenomenon (UAP) report. Instead, reports enter official [records]({{ 'records/' | relative_url }}) only when they intersect with the responsibilities of a particular agency. That means the Island's official UFO record is best understood as a filtered record rather than a complete one. Aviation authorities document events that may affect flight safety, while police services respond when public safety, criminal activity or security appear to be involved. Many ordinary reports of unexplained lights therefore never become part of any official archive at all. This distinction is essential when interpreting Prince Edward Island's historical record because an absence of official files does not necessarily indicate an absence of sightings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[Innovation Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Innovation CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## Which Island UFO reports enter official systems?

Official records exist because government agencies perform specific operational duties, not because they are attempting to catalogue unexplained aerial phenomena comprehensively. The Office of the Chief Science Advisor's Sky Canada Project found that Canada's reporting landscape remains fragmented, with multiple organisations receiving reports according to their own mandates rather than through a unified national system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[Innovation Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Innovation CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## When aviation authorities record an unexplained sighting

Transport Canada's Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS) provides one of the few continuing official pathways through which unusual aerial observations may become publicly documented. However, CADORS is an aviation occurrence database, not a UFO database. Its purpose is to record incidents affecting civil aviation safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[Innovation Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Innovation CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

A report is most likely to enter CADORS when:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a pilot observes an unidentified object during flight;
* air traffic controllers receive reports requiring operational attention;
* an object appears capable of interfering with aircraft operations;
* flight crews submit a report through established aviation procedures.

</div>

The reporting chain itself acts as a filter. Pilots normally report unusual observations to the appropriate air traffic service unit or flight information centre. Those observations become Aviation Occurrence Reports before Transport Canada decides whether they meet the criteria for publication within CADORS. Depending on the circumstances, entries may appear under broader operational categories that also include weather balloons, meteors, rockets or intelligence-related sighting procedures rather than standing alone as "UFO" reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[Innovation Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Innovation CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

This filtering process has two important consequences for Prince Edward Island.

First, aviation records often contain unusually precise information. Flight number, altitude, weather, location, timing and communications may all be documented because those details are operationally important. Such reports can therefore be among the strongest contemporary sources available.

Second, aviation databases inevitably exclude many civilian observations that never affected aircraft. A bright light seen from a beach or country road may attract considerable local discussion while generating no aviation documentation whatsoever.

## Why police reports depend on perceived risk

Police organisations approach unexplained aerial reports very differently from aviation authorities.

The Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP), which provides provincial policing in Prince Edward Island, investigates matters relating to crime and public safety rather than unexplained aerial phenomena themselves. The Sky Canada Project found that Canadian police services generally lack standardised systems for collecting or categorising UAP reports, and they have no consistent national policy for handling such incidents. During consultations, the RCMP indicated that a dedicated reporting office could help redirect non-criminal reports while allowing police to remain focused on policing responsibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[Innovation Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Innovation CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

In practice, whether an Island sighting generates a police record often depends less on how unusual the object appears than on whether someone believes it presents an immediate risk.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* suspected drones operating illegally;
* objects apparently threatening aircraft or vehicles;
* reports connected with emergency calls;
* concerns about trespassing, fires or explosions;
* possible criminal behaviour associated with unusual lights.

</div>

By contrast, someone reporting a distant silent light with no apparent danger may simply receive reassurance or advice without any extensive [investigation]({{ 'investigation/' | relative_url }}) or formal case file.

This means police records systematically favour sightings involving perceived hazards over unexplained observations that remained merely puzzling.

## What official silence can and cannot tell readers

One common misunderstanding is to assume that the absence of official records means nothing unusual occurred.

For Prince Edward Island, that conclusion is not justified.

Several mechanisms reduce the number of reports entering official systems:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* witnesses may never report the event;
* reports may be made only to local media, private researchers or online databases;
* agencies may determine that no operational response is required;
* records may exist within routine administrative files rather than as stand-alone UFO investigations;
* different organisations retain information using different indexing systems.

</div>

Library and Archives Canada illustrates this problem historically. Its federal UFO collection contains thousands of documents originating from the Department of National Defence, Department of Transport, National Research Council and RCMP, but the archive itself was assembled from agency records rather than from a single national investigation programme. Many files concern procedures or correspondence instead of individual sightings, while others lack complete location information, making provincial totals inherently incomplete.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

For that reason, official silence should normally be interpreted as evidence that no agency identified an operational reason to pursue the report—not as evidence that no witness observed anything unusual.

## Reading Prince Edward Island's official record carefully

Understanding these institutional filters helps explain why Prince Edward Island's official UFO history appears relatively sparse compared with some private databases or later surveys.

Official aviation records tend to contain the strongest technical detail because they originate from flight-safety procedures. Police files are more likely to exist when public safety concerns arise than when witnesses merely describe unexplained lights. Neither system was designed to measure the frequency of unusual aerial observations across the province.

Consequently, the Island's documented history reflects the priorities of aviation safety, policing and government administration as much as it reflects the underlying pattern of reported sightings.
