## Introduction

When a pilot reports an unidentified object or unusual light over [Saskatchewan]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-saskatchewans/' | relative_url }}), the report enters Canada's aviation safety system rather than a dedicated UFO investigation programme. The immediate priority is to protect aircraft and passengers, not to determine whether the object is extraordinary. A cockpit report may therefore move from air traffic control to NAV CANADA and then to Transport Canada's Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS), where it becomes part of the country's official aviation occurrence record. That record confirms that a report was received and logged, but it does not verify what the object actually was. This distinction is essential when interpreting Saskatchewan pilot [sightings]({{ 'sightings/' | relative_url }}) within the province's UFO history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ISED Canada Management of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in Canada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## What pilots and controllers record during a sighting

A pilot who encounters an unidentified object over Saskatchewan will normally report it to the nearest air traffic control (ATC) unit, flight service station or other NAV CANADA facility while still managing the flight safely. Controllers are concerned first with operational questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Where was the object?
* At what altitude and heading?
* Did it create a collision risk?
* Was it moving like another aircraft, a balloon, a drone or a meteor?
* Did radar or another aircraft detect the same thing?
* Did the sighting require avoiding action or affect air traffic?

</div>

These details are collected because they help maintain safe separation between aircraft and identify possible hazards in Canadian airspace. The reporting process is designed to capture operational facts rather than opinions about what the object might be.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ISED Canada Management of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in Canada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025</span></span></span>

In Saskatchewan, this process is the same whether the sighting occurs near Regina or Saskatoon, over northern wilderness, or in controlled en route airspace. The province's large areas of sparsely populated terrain can make cockpit observations particularly valuable because there may be few or no ground witnesses.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Saskatchewan_59faf2_official_ufo_records_56a07f_pilot_sighting_repor_0836b1-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Pilot Reports illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Saskatchewan_59faf2_official_ufo_records_56a07f_pilot_sighting_repor_0836b1-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Saskatchewan_59faf2_official_ufo_records_56a07f_pilot_sighting_repor_0836b1-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

## How reports move from air traffic control to Transport Canada

The path from cockpit observation to official record follows an established [aviation]({{ 'aviation-fa870c/' | relative_url }}) reporting chain rather than a UFO-specific system.

1. **The pilot reports the sighting** to an air traffic controller or flight service specialist.
2. **NAV CANADA documents the occurrence**, usually through an Aviation Occurrence Report containing the operational details supplied by the flight crew and controllers.
3. **The report is forwarded to Transport Canada**, whose CADORS team reviews the information.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Saskatchewan_59faf2_official_ufo_records_56a07f_pilot_sighting_repor_0836b1-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Pilot Reports illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Saskatchewan_59faf2_official_ufo_records_56a07f_pilot_sighting_repor_0836b1-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Saskatchewan_59faf2_official_ufo_records_56a07f_pilot_sighting_repor_0836b1-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

4. **Transport Canada creates a CADORS entry** if the occurrence meets the reporting criteria for the database. [ISED Canada](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada)

The Sky Canada Project found that pilot reports of unidentified aerial phenomena generally follow this route. Reports submitted directly by members of the public through Transport Canada's aviation incident reporting system may also appear in CADORS if they satisfy the database's inclusion criteria, although cockpit reports remain the most common aviation pathway. [ISED Canada](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada)

## Why a CADORS entry is not a verified identification

A common misunderstanding is that an official database entry means the government has confirmed an unidentified craft or investigated an extraordinary event.

CADORS does neither.

Transport Canada explicitly describes CADORS as a preliminary reporting system. Entries are intended to distribute timely information about aviation occurrences, and the published details are considered preliminary, unsubstantiated and subject to later revision. The database exists primarily to improve aviation safety rather than to establish the final explanation for every incident. [Transport Canada](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/aviation-publications/civil-aviation-daily-occurrence-reporting-system-cadors-manual-tp-4044)

For reports involving unusual aerial observations, CADORS may classify the event under categories that include weather balloons, meteors, rockets or UFO/CIRVIS sightings, depending on the available information. These categories describe the nature of the report received rather than the confirmed identity of the object. An unidentified object can therefore remain unidentified in the database without implying anything extraordinary. [ISED Canada](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada)

## What happens after the report is logged

Most pilot sightings do not trigger a dedicated investigation once immediate flight safety concerns have passed.

If controllers conclude that there is no continuing operational hazard, the report may simply remain as an occurrence record. Where additional evidence exists—such as radar information, multiple aircraft observations or possible airspace security implications—other organisations may become involved. Depending on the circumstances, this can include Transport Canada specialists, NAV CANADA operational staff or, in cases involving air defence or unidentified radar contacts, military authorities and NORAD. Whether additional investigation occurs depends on the safety or security implications rather than the unexplained nature of the sighting itself. [ISED Canada+2Transport Canada](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada)

The Sky Canada Project noted that reports classified as unidentified aerial phenomena represent only a tiny fraction of all CADORS occurrences. Because most do not present an ongoing aviation safety issue, they rarely receive extensive follow-up aimed specifically at identifying the observed object. [ISED Canada](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada)

## What this means for Saskatchewan UFO history

For researchers studying Saskatchewan's aviation-related UFO reports, a CADORS record is valuable because it establishes that trained aviation personnel reported an unusual event through an official safety system.

It can provide:

* a precise date and time;
* aircraft type and flight phase; [* approximate location and altitude;](#endnote-10 "Endnote 10")
* operational effects on the flight;
* controller communications; and
* references to any follow-up within the aviation system.

These details often make pilot cases more precisely documented than many civilian sightings. However, they should not be treated as official confirmation that the reported object was an unknown craft. The database records the occurrence, not the final explanation. Some entries are later attributed to conventional aircraft, astronomical objects, balloons or other identifiable causes, while others remain unresolved because the available evidence is insufficient to reach a firm conclusion. [Transport Canada](https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/aviation-publications/civil-aviation-daily-occurrence-reporting-system-cadors-manual-tp-4044)

Within the broader history of Saskatchewan UFO reporting, pilot reports therefore occupy a distinctive position: they enter official federal aviation records through well-established safety procedures, giving historians and researchers reliable documentation of what was reported, while leaving open the separate question of what was actually seen.
