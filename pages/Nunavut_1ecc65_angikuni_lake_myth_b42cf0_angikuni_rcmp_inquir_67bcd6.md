## Introduction

The short answer is no: the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP) did not confirm that an Inuit settlement mysteriously disappeared at Angikuni Lake. The surviving police record points in the opposite direction. After newspaper stories circulated in late 1930, the RCMP examined the claim and reportedly concluded that it had **“no foundation”**. Later RCMP historical statements have remained consistent, saying there is no evidence that such a disappearance occurred and that patrol records, local trappers and missionaries recorded nothing unusual in the area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcmp.ca/en/history-rcmp/fun-facts-and-urban-legends?lang=en&amp;wbdisable=false" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcmp.ca">[RCMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcmp.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fun facts and urban legends &#124; Royal Canadian Mounted Police</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fun facts and urban legends &#124; Royal Canadian Mounted PoliceMarch 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 10, 2025</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because many later retellings—especially those linked to UFO and paranormal literature—cite an RCMP investigation as though it verified the story. In reality, the available evidence indicates that the police investigated the newspaper report and rejected its central claim. Within [Nunavut]({{ 'what-really-lies-behind-nunavuts-ufo/' | relative_url }})'s UFO folklore, the Angikuni case is therefore notable not because it was officially confirmed, but because an official inquiry has repeatedly been misrepresented as supporting a legend.

## Did the RCMP Confirm the Angikuni Disappearance?

The earliest widely circulated version of the story prompted inquiries to the RCMP after it appeared in newspapers during late 1930. According to historical accounts of the investigation, Sergeant J. Nelson, based at The Pas, prepared an internal report in January 1931.

His reported conclusion was straightforward: he could find **"no foundation for this story."** Rather than documenting a vanished settlement, the inquiry questioned important parts of the original newspaper account. Among other points, Nelson reportedly noted that Joe Labelle—the trapper presented as the principal witness—had only recently obtained his first trapping licence, raising doubts about claims that he was a long-established traveller familiar with the district. Nelson also reportedly identified one published photograph as unrelated to the alleged event and described journalist Emmett E. Kelleher as known for producing colourful stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angikuni_Lake" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Angikuni Lake</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Angikuni Lake</span></span></span>

Although the complete original police file has not become a widely published archival document, the reported findings have been consistently repeated in historical summaries and have never been contradicted by the RCMP itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angikuni_Lake" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Angikuni Lake</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Angikuni Lake</span></span></span>

## What Police Records Reportedly Concluded

The RCMP's own historical guidance focuses less on individual details than on the absence of supporting evidence. Its position can be summarised in three key findings:

* **No evidence supported the reported disappearance.**
* **Mounted Police patrols recorded no unusual incident in the area.**
* **Local trappers and missionaries likewise reported nothing exceptional.**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcmp.ca/en/history-rcmp/fun-facts-and-urban-legends?lang=en&amp;wbdisable=false" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcmp.ca">[RCMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcmp.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fun facts and urban legends &#124; Royal Canadian Mounted Police</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fun facts and urban legends &#124; Royal Canadian Mounted PoliceMarch 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 10, 2025</span></span></span>

This observation addresses the expanded versions of the legend, in which the number of missing people grew dramatically over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcmp.ca/en/history-rcmp/fun-facts-and-urban-legends?lang=en&amp;wbdisable=false" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcmp.ca">[RCMP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcmp.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fun facts and urban legends &#124; Royal Canadian Mounted Police</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fun facts and urban legends &#124; Royal Canadian Mounted PoliceMarch 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 10, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why Investigating a Story Is Not Confirming a Disappearance

One of the most persistent misunderstandings surrounding Angikuni Lake is the assumption that an RCMP investigation automatically validates the original claim.

Police investigations often begin because a report has been made, not because authorities believe the report is true. In the Angikuni case, the existence of an inquiry demonstrates that officials examined the allegation. It does **not** mean they confirmed that an Inuit community vanished.

This distinction has become blurred because many books, documentaries and websites mention only that the RCMP "investigated" the incident while omitting the reported conclusion that the story lacked factual support. Once separated from its outcome, the investigation itself has been mistakenly presented as evidence for the legend rather than evidence against it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angikuni_Lake" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Angikuni Lake</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Angikuni Lake</span></span></span>

For readers interested in UFO history, this is a useful reminder that official involvement should always be distinguished from official endorsement.
