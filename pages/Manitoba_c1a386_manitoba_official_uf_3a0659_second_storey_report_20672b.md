## Introduction

Project Second Storey is often remembered as one of Canada's earliest official efforts to examine reports of unidentified flying objects, but one of its most lasting ambitions was administrative rather than investigative. Between 1952 and 1954, the committee designed a standard reporting form intended to ensure that sightings received by different government departments were recorded in the same way. The idea was straightforward: if every report contained comparable information, officials could more easily distinguish genuine aviation or defence concerns from misidentified natural phenomena. In practice, however, the form was never adopted widely enough to become Canada's national reporting standard. That failure left UFO reports from [Manitoba]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-manitobas-ufo/' | relative_url }}) and elsewhere scattered across multiple agencies, making later investigation and historical research far more difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

For Manitoba, this matters because many of the province's best-known cases—including later incidents such as [Falcon Lake]({{ 'falcon-lake/' | relative_url }})—entered a fragmented reporting system rather than a single national archive. Understanding why Project Second Storey's reporting system never took hold helps explain why Canada's UFO records remain incomplete and why reconstructing federal investigations often requires consulting several separate collections.

## Who served on Project Second Storey?

Project Second Storey was established in 1952 by the Defence Research Board, Canada's military scientific organisation within the Department of National Defence. Rather than operating as an independent investigative unit, it functioned as an interdepartmental committee bringing together expertise from science, defence and intelligence.

The committee was chaired by astronomer Dr Peter Millman of the National Research Council (then associated with the Dominion Observatory) and included representatives from:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the Defence Research Board;
* the Royal Canadian Air Force and military intelligence;
* the Department of National Defence;
* the Department of Transport; and
* other federal organisations with responsibility for aviation or security matters.

</div>

The committee met only six times between 1952 and 1954. Its members generally approached UFO reports from a scientific and national-security perspective rather than assuming extraordinary explanations. Their objective was to determine whether sightings represented aircraft, atmospheric phenomena, defence threats or other identifiable events requiring government attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## What the standard reporting form was meant to fix

When Project Second Storey began its work, Canada had no consistent process for recording UFO reports. Witnesses might contact local police, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, the Department of Transport, military authorities or another government office. Each organisation tended to collect different information, using its own paperwork and priorities.

The committee believed this inconsistency made meaningful analysis almost impossible. Reports often lacked basic observational details or omitted information that would help investigators compare one sighting with another.

The proposed standard reporting form attempted to solve this by requiring consistent factual observations, including matters such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* date and precise time;
* location;
* direction of travel;
* weather and visibility;
* duration of the observation;
* appearance, brightness and movement of the object;
* number and experience of witnesses; and
* any corroborating aviation or radar information where available.

</div>

The emphasis was on measurable observations rather than speculation. Instead of encouraging lengthy narratives about what witnesses believed they had seen, the form sought objective information that could be compared across multiple reports and examined by scientists or aviation specialists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canadaufohistory.com/1947-1954/project-second-storey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canadaufohistory.com">[Canada UFO History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canadaufohistory.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada UFO HistoryCanada UFO History - Project Second Storey...</span></span></span>

This approach reflected the committee's broader philosophy: before asking whether an incident was unusual, investigators needed reliable, comparable data.

## Why the reporting system never became national policy

Although the reporting form represented a practical administrative improvement, it never evolved into a mandatory government-wide reporting system.

Several factors contributed to its limited adoption.

First, Project Second Storey itself was short-lived. After six meetings, committee members concluded that reported UFOs neither presented a significant national-security threat nor justified a major scientific research programme. As a result, enthusiasm for building a permanent reporting infrastructure diminished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

Second, no single department was given lasting responsibility for collecting every Canadian report. Different organisations continued receiving sightings according to their existing responsibilities. Police handled public complaints, aviation authorities dealt with flight safety, military organisations considered defence implications, and later the National Research Council accepted many public reports. None became the definitive national clearing house.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

Third, because the reporting form was never consistently required across departments, many officials continued using local procedures or informal correspondence instead of the standardised format. As a consequence, reports varied considerably in quality and completeness.

The result was not the absence of records but the absence of uniform records.

## How limited adoption weakened Canada's UFO archive

The consequences of this administrative failure became clearer over subsequent decades.

Instead of a single searchable national collection created from identical reporting forms, Canada's historical UFO records accumulated through multiple institutional channels. Files eventually originated from organisations including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the Royal Canadian Mounted Police;
* the Department of National Defence;
* the Department of Transport; and
* the National Research Council.

</div>

Because each agency recorded information differently, researchers frequently encounter duplicate files, incomplete investigations, inconsistent terminology and varying levels of detail. Modern historians often have to reconstruct individual incidents by comparing documents from several archives rather than consulting one complete case file.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

The Office of the Chief Science Advisor's Sky Canada Project, published in 2025, identified this fragmented historical reporting system as one of the recurring weaknesses in Canada's handling of unidentified aerial phenomena. Looking back, it noted that Project Second Storey's reporting form was created specifically to improve consistency but was "never broadly adopted or used," leaving responsibility to drift between departments over subsequent decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why this mattered for Manitoba

For Manitoba, the consequences were practical rather than theoretical.

Many provincial reports entered whichever federal organisation happened to receive them first. A report might begin with local police, be forwarded to the RCMP, copied to military authorities because aircraft were mentioned, or later reach the National Research Council if scientific identification was sought. Different agencies often retained different parts of the documentary trail.

This fragmented system helps explain why well-known Manitoba cases survive today in several archival collections rather than in one complete investigation file. Researchers examining incidents such as Falcon Lake often need to compare records held by different departments to understand how officials responded and what evidence each organisation considered.

Project Second Storey's unrealised reporting system therefore influenced not only obscure sightings but also some of Canada's most studied cases.

## The lasting significance

Project Second Storey's reporting form was an early attempt to solve a genuine administrative problem rather than to prove or disprove extraordinary claims. Its designers recognised that meaningful investigation depends on consistent evidence collection. In that respect, the proposal anticipated modern principles of incident reporting used in aviation and other technical fields.

Its failure was not that the form itself was technically flawed, but that no lasting government structure required its consistent use. Canada continued collecting UFO reports for decades, yet without the unified reporting system that Project Second Storey had envisioned. The surviving archival record consequently became extensive but uneven, preserving thousands of documents while making comprehensive national analysis more difficult than it might otherwise have been. This fragmented legacy remains central to understanding both Manitoba's place in Canada's UFO history and the broader evolution of Canadian government investigations into unidentified aerial phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>
