## Introduction

One of the most discussed features of the January 2023 Canadian North sighting near Yellowknife is that neither the flight crew's onboard traffic system nor air traffic controllers reported matching aircraft. At first glance, that may appear to rule out an ordinary aviation explanation. In practice, however, the absence of a radar or traffic-system contact is informative without being decisive. It weakens the idea that the lights were another conventionally tracked aircraft operating normally in controlled airspace, but it does not eliminate other possibilities such as poorly located distant lights, balloons, drones, satellites, optical effects or aircraft that were not generating the particular surveillance data available to the crew and controllers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cabinradio.ca/120760/news/yellowknife/canadian-north-crew-reports-lights-in-sky-over-yellowknife/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cabinradio.ca">[cabinradio.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cabinradio.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cabin Radio Canadian North crew reports ‘lights in sky’ over Yellowknife</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cabin RadioCanadian North crew reports ‘lights in sky’ over YellowknifeFebruary 11, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 11, 2023</span></span></span>

## Why No Radar Return Did Not Settle the Case

During the radio exchange, the Canadian North crew told Yellowknife controllers that they could see two bright lights but were not receiving corresponding targets on the aircraft's Traffic Collision Avoidance System (TCAS). Controllers replied that they likewise had no obvious matching surveillance contact and confirmed that area controllers had no reported traffic carrying out manoeuvres nearby. The crew stressed that the lights posed no immediate collision hazard but wanted to know whether any known aircraft matched what they were observing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cabinradio.ca/120760/news/yellowknife/canadian-north-crew-reports-lights-in-sky-over-yellowknife/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cabinradio.ca">[Cabin Radio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cabinradio.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cabin Radio Canadian North crew reports ‘lights in sky’ over Yellowknife</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cabin RadioCanadian North crew reports ‘lights in sky’ over YellowknifeFebruary 11, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 11, 2023</span></span></span>

That combination makes the sighting more interesting than an ordinary visual misidentification. If another airliner had been operating in the reported location using a functioning transponder, both TCAS and modern air traffic surveillance would normally be expected to provide some indication of its presence. The absence of such indications therefore weighs against the simplest explanation of "another scheduled aircraft."

However, it is only one piece of evidence. Surveillance systems are designed around known aircraft broadcasting recognised signals or reflecting radar energy under particular conditions. They are not universal detectors of every illuminated object visible in the night sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/reference-centre/advisory-circulars/advisory-circular-ac-no" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada Advisory Circular (AC) No.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada Advisory Circular (AC) No.</span></span></span>

## What Aircraft Traffic-Alert Systems Can and Cannot Detect

TCAS is a collision-avoidance system rather than a general object detector. It interrogates compatible aircraft transponders and warns crews about aircraft that meet specific equipment and operational conditions. It does not detect every airborne object simply because it is visible from the cockpit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/reference-centre/advisory-circulars/advisory-circular-ac-no" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada Advisory Circular (AC) No.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada Advisory Circular (AC) No.</span></span></span>

In the context of the Yellowknife sighting, several important limitations matter:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **No compatible transponder, no TCAS target.** Objects without an operating aviation transponder—including balloons, many drones and satellites—will not appear.
* **Not every aircraft necessarily appears.** Equipment failures, disabled transponders or aircraft operating outside required surveillance standards can reduce or eliminate TCAS detection.
* **TCAS is built for collision avoidance.** Its purpose is identifying aircraft that could present a traffic conflict, not cataloguing every light visible from the cockpit.

</div>

Consequently, the crew's statement that nothing appeared on TCAS is significant because it argues against a normally equipped nearby aircraft, but it cannot identify what the lights actually were.

## Why Surveillance Coverage May Miss Real Objects

Ground surveillance is also more complicated than the phrase "nothing on radar" sometimes suggests.

Modern Canadian air traffic surveillance increasingly combines conventional radar with Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B), in which equipped aircraft broadcast their own position. Space-based ADS-B has greatly expanded surveillance across Canada's northern airspace, but the capabilities available to controllers depend on aircraft equipage, system architecture and the operational environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.navcanada.ca/en/flight-planning/service-notices/2023-08-10-national-.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: navcanada.ca">[navcanada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">navcanada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2023 08 10 national.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NAV CANADANAV CANADA 2023-08-10 NationalAugust 10, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 10, 2023</span></span></span>

Several factors can therefore prevent a visible object from producing an identifiable surveillance track:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the object may not transmit ADS-B or a compatible transponder signal;
* it may have too little radar cross-section for reliable conventional detection;
* it may be outside the geometry needed for dependable surveillance identification;
* it may not be an aircraft at all.

</div>

These limitations explain why investigators generally avoid treating the absence of surveillance data as proof that no object existed. Instead, they ask a narrower question: would a conventional aircraft operating as expected have been detected? In the Yellowknife case, the answer appears closer to "probably yes" than "certainly yes", leaving room for both conventional and unresolved interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/reference-centre/advisory-circulars/advisory-circular-ac-no" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada Advisory Circular (AC) No.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada Advisory Circular (AC) No.</span></span></span>

## How Uncertain Location Estimates Affect Radar Searches

Another difficulty comes from the crew's own changing estimates of where the lights were located.

During the approach, the reported position shifted from east of Yellowknife to above the aircraft and later to roughly 10–20 nautical miles north-west of the airport, with an estimated altitude around Flight Level 270. Such changes are not unusual during night observations. Without knowing the true distance to a light, even experienced pilots can misjudge its altitude and horizontal position while both observer and possible source continue moving.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cabinradio.ca/120760/news/yellowknife/canadian-north-crew-reports-lights-in-sky-over-yellowknife/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cabinradio.ca">[Cabin Radio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cabinradio.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cabin Radio Canadian North crew reports ‘lights in sky’ over Yellowknife</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cabin RadioCanadian North crew reports ‘lights in sky’ over YellowknifeFebruary 11, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 11, 2023</span></span></span>

This uncertainty affects radar interpretation in several ways.

If investigators search the wrong volume of airspace because the estimated position is inaccurate, an otherwise relevant radar contact may appear unrelated. Conversely, if the lights were actually much farther away than believed, they may never have occupied the region being checked by controllers in the first place.

The uncertainty therefore works in two directions: it reduces confidence in any specific altitude or distance estimate, while also making it harder to conclude that surveillance systems definitely searched the correct location.

## What the Missing Radar Contact Actually Tells Us

Taken on its own, the lack of a matching TCAS target and the absence of an obvious surveillance contact make the ordinary-airliner explanation less persuasive. They indicate that controllers and the flight crew were not overlooking a routine, identified aircraft conducting normal operations near Yellowknife.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cabinradio.ca/120760/news/yellowknife/canadian-north-crew-reports-lights-in-sky-over-yellowknife/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cabinradio.ca">[Cabin Radio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cabinradio.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cabin Radio Canadian North crew reports ‘lights in sky’ over Yellowknife</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cabin RadioCanadian North crew reports ‘lights in sky’ over YellowknifeFebruary 11, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 11, 2023</span></span></span>

They do not, however, demonstrate that the lights represented an unknown aircraft or an extraordinary phenomenon. The evidence remains compatible with several possibilities that would not necessarily produce a radar or traffic-system return, including balloons, some drones, distant illuminated objects, satellites seen under favourable lighting conditions, or optical effects whose apparent motion differed from their true motion.

Within the broader history of aviation-related UFO reports in the [Northwest Territories]({{ 'northwest-territories/' | relative_url }}), this is why the Yellowknife incident remains noteworthy but unresolved. The absence of matching surveillance data strengthens the case for taking the crew's observation seriously, yet it falls short of identifying what they saw. The radar evidence narrows the range of conventional explanations without eliminating them altogether.
