## Introduction

In [Alberta]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-albertas-ufo/' | relative_url }})'s government UFO files, **"unresolved" does not mean officials concluded that an extraordinary craft had been seen**. In almost every case, it means investigators could not confidently identify what was reported because the available evidence was too limited, incomplete or contradictory. That distinction is central to understanding what Canadian government records actually show. The files demonstrate that unusual aerial observations were documented and sometimes examined by public authorities, but an unresolved outcome normally reflects uncertainty rather than confirmation of an exotic explanation. Canadian archival guidance also warns that many historical files are incomplete, undated or lack precise locations, making firm conclusions difficult even decades later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.caMarch 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2026</span></span></span>

For readers interested in Alberta's UFO history, this means an unresolved file should be read as the end of an investigation, not the beginning of a proven mystery.

## Does unresolved mean officials ruled out everything?

No. An unresolved classification usually means investigators **could not rule enough things in or out**.

Government agencies responsible for recording unusual aerial reports—including the Department of National Defence, Department of Transport, Royal Canadian Mounted Police and National Research Council—worked with whatever information witnesses supplied. Sometimes that information was detailed enough to identify a meteor, aircraft, balloon or astronomical object. In other cases, there simply was not enough evidence to reach a reliable conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.caMarch 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2026</span></span></span>

An unresolved report therefore says:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the observation was considered insufficient for a confident identification;
* investigators did not have enough evidence to support an ordinary explanation with certainty; and
* investigators also did not obtain evidence proving an extraordinary explanation.

</div>

This middle ground is often misunderstood. "Unresolved" is fundamentally an administrative judgement about the quality of the evidence rather than a declaration that something impossible occurred.

## Why ordinary explanations can remain unproven

Many Alberta reports involve brief, one-time observations of lights or objects in the sky. Even if a conventional explanation is likely, investigators may have lacked enough information to prove it.

Common reasons include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Very short observations.** A light seen for only a few seconds may provide too little information for identification.
* **Single witnesses.** Without independent observations, photographs or radar data, investigators cannot compare accounts.
* **Missing timing information.** Precise times are essential when checking astronomical objects, aircraft movements or meteor activity.
* **Limited weather records.** Cloud cover, atmospheric conditions and visibility may not have been documented in enough detail.
* **No physical evidence.** Most reports consist only of witness descriptions rather than recoverable objects or measurable traces.
* **Delayed reporting.** Reports submitted days or weeks later make reconstruction much harder.

</div>

These limitations explain why an investigator may decline to identify an object while also declining to endorse extraordinary interpretations.

## What evidence investigators often lacked

Historical Canadian UFO files, including those containing Alberta incidents, rarely resemble the detailed investigations portrayed in popular culture.

Instead, many consist of correspondence, report forms and brief summaries. Some include sketches or follow-up letters, while others contain only a witness statement and administrative routing between departments. Library and Archives Canada also notes that many archived records lack complete dates or locations, and that searches by place or date produce only partial results because catalogue entries reflect whatever information appeared in the original paperwork.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.caMarch 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2026</span></span></span>

Evidence commonly missing from unresolved files includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* corroborating witnesses from different locations;
* air traffic or military tracking data;
* photographs or film of sufficient quality;
* instrument measurements;
* detailed interview transcripts;
* follow-up field investigations completed close to the event.

</div>

Without these elements, investigators frequently reached the only defensible conclusion available: the object remained unidentified on the evidence available.

## How unresolved differs from unexplained and confirmed

These terms are often used interchangeably in popular discussions, but they describe different ideas.

TermWhat it usually means in government records**Unresolved**Available evidence was insufficient for a reliable identification.**Unexplained**No satisfactory conventional explanation was established after investigation, although this still does not prove an extraordinary cause.**Confirmed**The evidence positively identifies what was observed—for example, an aircraft, meteor, balloon or another recognised phenomenon.

An unresolved report is therefore not automatically an unexplained phenomenon in the strongest sense. It may simply be a case where investigators judged that the evidence was too weak to support any firm conclusion.

Likewise, it should not be interpreted as confirmation of an alien spacecraft, secret technology or another extraordinary explanation. Canadian government records do not use unresolved as a synonym for those conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.caMarch 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2026</span></span></span>

## What this means for Alberta's UFO history

Alberta contains military facilities, busy commercial aviation routes, extensive rural areas and large expanses of dark sky. Those conditions naturally produce a mixture of genuine aircraft sightings, astronomical observations, weather-related phenomena and occasional reports that cannot be confidently identified after the fact.

When readers encounter an Alberta government file labelled unresolved, the most accurate interpretation is modest:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the report entered the official record;
* someone considered it worth documenting or assessing;
* the available evidence did not support a confident identification; and
* the investigation ended without enough information to move beyond uncertainty.

</div>

That makes unresolved files historically interesting because they show the limits of available evidence and investigative procedures. They do **not** demonstrate that officials secretly verified extraordinary craft or ruled out every conventional possibility.

## Reading unresolved files carefully

The most reliable way to interpret Alberta's historical UFO records is to separate three different questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Did someone report something unusual?** Government files often show that they did.
2. **Was the report investigated?** Sometimes yes, although the depth of investigation varied considerably.
3. **Did investigators establish what the object was?** In unresolved cases, the answer is simply that they could not do so with confidence.

</div>

This distinction helps avoid a common misunderstanding. A government archive preserves what officials recorded and how they responded. It does not automatically validate the witness's interpretation.
