## Introduction

One of the most disputed aspects of the 1967 [Falcon Lake]({{ 'falcon-lake/' | relative_url }}) incident is not the original medical treatment but the photographs taken many months later. While Stefan Michalak was genuinely examined for abdominal burns shortly after his reported encounter near Falcon Lake, [Manitoba]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-manitobas-ufo/' | relative_url }}), the famous images showing a neat grid of evenly spaced circular marks were not taken immediately after the event. They were produced in January 1968, around eight months later, after Michalak claimed the burns had returned. That timing became crucial because the later photographs differ markedly from the injuries documented in May 1967, and subsequent medical opinion questioned whether the new lesions represented the original injury at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

Understanding this distinction is essential when assessing the [physical evidence]({{ 'physical-evidence/' | relative_url }}). The later photographs are among the most widely reproduced images associated with the Falcon Lake case, yet they have often been presented as though they depict the original wounds. The historical record paints a more complicated picture.

## How the early injuries were described

The earliest descriptions of Michalak's [injuries]({{ 'injuries/' | relative_url }}) came from medical examinations, police observations and photographs taken within days of the reported encounter.

Those contemporary records consistently describe [burns]({{ 'burns/' | relative_url }}) that were irregular rather than geometrically precise. Medical accounts referred to several rounded or blotchy burns resembling superficial thermal injuries. Police officers likened the affected area to a severe localised sunburn, while photographs taken on 23 May 1967 by civilian investigator J. B. Thompson show uneven lesions of differing size and spacing rather than a perfect grid.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because later retellings frequently merged two different sets of evidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **May 1967:** irregular burns observed shortly after the reported incident.
* **January 1968:** highly regular rows of evenly spaced circular lesions.
* **Later publications:** the January photographs were often reproduced without explaining that they documented a later development rather than the original injury.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

</div>

The confusion has shaped public perception of Falcon Lake for decades. Readers encountering only the famous grid photographs may reasonably assume they show the immediate aftermath of the encounter when they do not.

## Why the January 1968 photographs differ

In January 1968 Michalak contacted newspapers again, saying the burns had reappeared. The resulting photographs showed an unusually regular pattern of dots across his abdomen, far more uniform than the injuries documented in May.

The visual difference is striking. Instead of irregular patches of inflamed skin, the later images display small circular lesions arranged in straight horizontal and vertical rows with nearly equal spacing. This appearance corresponded more closely to the vent pattern Michalak had described on the alleged craft than the original medical photographs ever had.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

Because these images were visually dramatic, they gradually displaced the earlier photographs in books, documentaries and popular articles. As a result, many accounts unintentionally created the impression that investigators had documented a perfect grid immediately after the event.

Modern historical reviews of the case have highlighted this as an important source of misunderstanding. The January images represent a later episode rather than the injuries originally examined by doctors in May 1967.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theblackarchive.net/en/case/16" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackarchive.net">[검은 기록 보관소]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackarchive.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">검은 기록 보관소The Falcon Lake Incident — CASE FILE 016 &#124; The Black Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">검은 기록 보관소The Falcon Lake Incident — CASE FILE 016 &#124; The Black Archive</span></span></span>

## What the factitial diagnosis does and does not mean

Perhaps the most debated medical opinion came later in 1968 when Michalak underwent psychiatric evaluation at the Mayo Clinic.

The examining psychiatrist noted that the skin lesions had been diagnosed as **"obviously factitial."** In medical terminology, a *factitial* lesion is one produced or maintained through external manipulation rather than arising naturally from disease or injury alone. The term describes the apparent mechanism of the skin damage; it does not by itself determine motive or prove deliberate fraud.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

Equally important is what the Mayo report did **not** conclude. The psychiatrist also wrote that he could find **no overt evidence of significant mental or emotional illness**. In other words:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the report questioned the origin of the later skin lesions;
* it did not diagnose psychosis or delusional illness;
* it did not claim to have solved the Falcon Lake incident;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>
* it did not establish that every aspect of Michalak's account had been fabricated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

</div>

This nuance is frequently lost in simplified retellings. Some sceptical accounts portray the factitial diagnosis as proof the entire case was a hoax, while some UFO literature ignores or minimises the diagnosis altogether. Neither interpretation accurately reflects the medical record.

## What this means for the physical evidence

The later photographs substantially weaken one frequently repeated argument for the Falcon Lake incident: that the famous grid pattern provides direct visual confirmation of Michalak's original account.

Instead, the evidence separates into two distinct categories.

The first consists of injuries observed shortly after the reported encounter. These were documented by physicians and investigators and appear to represent genuine superficial burns or burn-like lesions, although they do not identify their cause. The second consists of the much more famous January 1968 photographs, whose appearance differs significantly from the original injuries and whose associated medical assessment questioned whether they were naturally occurring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Lake_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Falcon Lake Incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Falcon Lake Incident</span></span></span>

For historians of Manitoba's UFO reports, this distinction is critical. It does not eliminate the possibility that Michalak suffered an unexplained injury in May 1967. It does, however, make the later checkerboard photographs far less reliable as evidence for reconstructing what happened at Falcon Lake.

## Why the distinction still matters

Within the broader dispute over burns, radioactivity and physical evidence, the later photographs illustrate how an already unusual case became more complicated over time.

The original burns remain part of the documented medical history of the Falcon Lake incident. The January 1968 photographs belong to a later phase in which new lesions appeared under disputed circumstances and received a diagnosis suggesting external production or maintenance.
