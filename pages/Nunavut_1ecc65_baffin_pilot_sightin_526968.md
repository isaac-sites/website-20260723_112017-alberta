## Introduction

The Baffin Island pilot sighting of 24 November 2018 is arguably [Nunavut]({{ 'what-really-lies-behind-nunavuts-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s best-documented modern UFO case, but “best-documented” is relative. A commercial flight crew reported a conspicuous, colour-changing light while flying towards the Mary River mine, and the observation entered Transport Canada’s Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System, known as CADORS. That gives the incident a reliable date, route, aircraft type and [official reporting]({{ 'official-reports/' | relative_url }}) trail. It does not, however, provide enough evidence to identify what the pilots saw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/pilots-report-ufo-sighting-over-nunavuts-northern-baffin-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nunatsiaq.com">[Nunatsiaq News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nunatsiaq.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">News Pilots report UFO sighting over Nunavut’s northern Baffin Island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The pilots were flying a Nolinor Aviation Boeing 737-200 jet, and reported that the sighting took place at about 8:30 p.m. local time...</span></span></span>

The surviving record contains no photograph, video, radar return, measured trajectory or published meteorological and astronomical reconstruction. Even the airline’s representative said the light might have been a star, meteor or another natural phenomenon. ## The flight, location and changing light

The aircraft was a Nolinor Aviation Boeing 737-200 travelling from Iqaluit to the Mary River iron-ore mine in northern Baffin Island. Contemporary reporting placed the sighting at approximately 8:30 p.m. local time. The route crossed a large, sparsely inhabited region in Arctic darkness, where crews depend heavily on instruments and air-traffic services but may have few ground lights or familiar visual reference points outside the cockpit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/65674pilots_spot_possible_ufo_above_nunavuts_northern_baffin_island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nunatsiaq.com">[Nunatsiaq News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nunatsiaq.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">65674pilots spot possible ufo above nunavuts northern baffin island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">24, as they flew from Iqaluit to the Mary River mine in north Baffin Island...</span></span></span>

The public account describes the observation as a bright or shining light that changed colour. Marco Prud’homme, then a Nolinor vice-president, said the pilot had described changes from red to green to white. Other summaries also included blue among the reported colours. Prud’homme did not portray the crew as claiming an exotic craft; he said the source might have been a star, a meteorite or “probably something natural”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uforum.blogspot.com/2018/12/nothing-too-baffling-at-baffin-island.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uforum.blogspot.com">[Uforum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uforum.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uforum Ufology Research: Nothing too baffling at Baffin Island [UPDATED</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">One of the CADORS categories is &quot;Weather balloon, rocket. meteor, CIRVIS/UFO.&quot;... &gt; Marco Prud’homme, Nolinor’s vice president, says the...</span></span></span>

That distinction is important. The report concerned a light, not a clearly observed structured vehicle. There is no published description of wings, a fuselage, windows, a metallic surface, exhaust, a defined outline or physical interaction with the aircraft. Nor does the surviving account establish that the light manoeuvred, accelerated or maintained a measured course. Much of the case’s later “UFO” character comes from its inability to be identified at the time and from the terminology used in the occurrence record, rather than from documented behaviour that was demonstrably beyond conventional aircraft or natural phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/pilots-report-ufo-sighting-over-nunavuts-northern-baffin-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nunatsiaq.com">[Nunatsiaq News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nunatsiaq.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">News Pilots report UFO sighting over Nunavut’s northern Baffin Island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The pilots were flying a Nolinor Aviation Boeing 737-200 jet, and reported that the sighting took place at about 8:30 p.m. local time...</span></span></span>

The reported colour changes are visually striking but not, by themselves, evidence of an object changing its emitted light. Stars viewed through turbulent layers of atmosphere can appear to fluctuate rapidly in brightness, apparent position and colour. This effect, called [scintillation]({{ 'scintillation/' | relative_url }}), is usually strongest when a star is low above the horizon because its light passes through more atmosphere. Cold winter conditions and strong temperature differences between air layers can make the effect especially noticeable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomy.com/science/simply-scintillating/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomy.com">[Astronomy Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Magazine Simply Scintillating &#124; Astronomy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy MagazineSimply Scintillating &#124; Astronomy.comOctober 24, 2011 — 18 May 2023 — Twinkling, the common term for stellar “scintillat...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2011</span></span></span>

A distant light seen from a moving cockpit can also be difficult to place in three dimensions. Without a known distance, apparent motion may arise from the aircraft’s own movement, changes in heading, cloud layers or shifts in the observer’s line of sight. This does not prove that the Baffin Island light was a star. It shows why descriptions such as “bright” and “changing colour” are insufficient unless accompanied by bearing changes, angular elevation, duration and comparison with the aircraft’s instruments.

## What the official report actually records

The sighting was communicated through Canadian aviation channels and entered into CADORS. That is the strongest evidential feature of the case. CADORS is Transport Canada’s principal system for collecting initial information about civil-aviation accidents, incidents and other occurrences. NAV CANADA supplies most of the source material, while information may also come from aircraft operators, the Transportation Safety Board, police and other agencies. Records undergo administrative processing and may be corrected or expanded after their initial creation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/publications/aviation-safety-letter/issue-2-2021/civil-aviation-daily-occurrence-reporting-system-cadors" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada The Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada The Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS</span></span></span>

The Baffin Island record used the description “weather balloon, meteor, rocket, CIRVIS/UFO”. CIRVIS stands for Communications Instructions for Reporting Vital Intelligence Sightings. Canadian aviation and defence procedures use the category for reports of unidentified or potentially significant objects and activities. Its presence confirms that the crew’s observation was passed into a recognised reporting process; it does not mean that officials determined the object was extraordinary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/pilots-report-ufo-sighting-over-nunavuts-northern-baffin-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nunatsiaq.com">[Nunatsiaq News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nunatsiaq.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">News Pilots report UFO sighting over Nunavut’s northern Baffin Island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The pilots were flying a Nolinor Aviation Boeing 737-200 jet, and reported that the sighting took place at about 8:30 p.m. local time...</span></span></span>

Transport Canada has since explicitly cautioned that “UFO” in CADORS can cover a wide range of possibilities, including drones, balloons, meteors, weather phenomena and birds. The term means unidentified within the report, not extraterrestrial. Transport Canada also describes CADORS information as preliminary, potentially unsubstantiated and subject to change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/binder/4-high-altitude-object-incidents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada4. This makes the record useful in one way and limited in another. It is useful because it anchors the story to a real flight and a near-contemporary operational report rather than an anonymous recollection posted years later. It is limited because CADORS is designed primarily to circulate safety information and identify aviation hazards. An entry is not equivalent to a completed forensic investigation, and the system does not guarantee that every unexplained light will receive radar analysis, astronomical modelling or interviews conducted to scientific standards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/publications/aviation-safety-letter/issue-2-2021/civil-aviation-daily-occurrence-reporting-system-cadors" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada The Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada The Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS</span></span></span>

The public report also appears to preserve only a small portion of the information normally needed to reconstruct a sighting. A strong aviation case would ideally include:

* exact time to the second and the duration of observation;
* aircraft position, altitude, heading and speed;
* the light’s bearing and elevation relative to the cockpit;
* whether it moved against the stars or remained fixed;
* accounts from each crew member recorded separately;
* weather, visibility, cloud and atmospheric conditions;
* radar, air-traffic-control and radio records;
* astronomical and satellite checks for that time and location.

</div>

The available reporting provides some basic flight and timing information but not this full dataset. Without it, apparently competing explanations cannot be tested properly.

## How strong is the witness evidence?

Commercial pilots are credible witnesses in the ordinary sense that they are trained to monitor airspace, weather and aircraft traffic. The observation was evidently notable enough for the crew to report it, and there is no obvious reason to think that the pilots invented the event. The report was also made close to the time of the sighting, reducing the risk of decades of memory distortion.

Pilot status nevertheless does not make visual identification infallible. Flight training is primarily concerned with safe aircraft operation, navigation, weather avoidance and recognition of operational hazards. It does not give a pilot perfect information about the distance or nature of an isolated point of light at night. An honest and experienced observer can describe an appearance accurately while misjudging its source.

The strongest interpretation of the testimony is therefore that the crew genuinely saw a conspicuous light that they could not identify. A weaker and unjustified interpretation would be that their occupation proves the light could not have been astronomical or atmospheric. The witnesses’ own cautious suggestion that it might have been natural weighs against later retellings that present the incident as an encounter with a clearly defined unknown craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uforum.blogspot.com/2018/12/nothing-too-baffling-at-baffin-island.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uforum.blogspot.com">[Uforum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uforum.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uforum Ufology Research: Nothing too baffling at Baffin Island [UPDATED</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">One of the CADORS categories is &quot;Weather balloon, rocket. meteor, CIRVIS/UFO.&quot;... &gt; Marco Prud’homme, Nolinor’s vice president, says the...</span></span></span>

News reports commonly referred to “pilots” or a flight crew, but the detailed public description was relayed through an airline executive and centred on what “the pilot” had told him. The surviving material does not publish separate statements from the captain and first officer showing whether both saw the same colours, position, movement and duration independently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/65674pilots_spot_possible_ufo_above_nunavuts_northern_baffin_island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nunatsiaq.com">[Nunatsiaq News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nunatsiaq.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">65674pilots spot possible ufo above nunavuts northern baffin island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">24, as they flew from Iqaluit to the Mary River mine in north Baffin Island...</span></span></span>

## Which explanations fit the limited facts?

A bright star affected by atmospheric scintillation is one of the more plausible possibilities because it directly matches the reported sequence of changing colours. A star low on the horizon can flash red, green, blue and white as turbulent air refracts different wavelengths towards the observer. If the light remained in roughly the same direction for an extended period, that would strengthen the astronomical explanation. The public account does not provide enough positional or duration data to carry out that test.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomy.com/science/simply-scintillating/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomy.com">[Astronomy Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Magazine Simply Scintillating &#124; Astronomy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy MagazineSimply Scintillating &#124; Astronomy.comOctober 24, 2011 — 18 May 2023 — Twinkling, the common term for stellar “scintillat...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2011</span></span></span>

A meteor is also mentioned in the original reporting, but its plausibility depends heavily on duration and motion. Meteors normally cross part of the sky over a short interval, often leaving a visible path or showing clear movement. A nearly stationary light watched for a long period would be difficult to reconcile with an ordinary meteor, whereas a brief bright object with changing colours could fit. Because the duration and apparent trajectory were not publicly preserved, the meteor suggestion can neither be confirmed nor confidently dismissed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uforum.blogspot.com/2018/12/nothing-too-baffling-at-baffin-island.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uforum.blogspot.com">[Uforum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uforum.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uforum Ufology Research: Nothing too baffling at Baffin Island [UPDATED</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">One of the CADORS categories is &quot;Weather balloon, rocket. meteor, CIRVIS/UFO.&quot;... &gt; Marco Prud’homme, Nolinor’s vice president, says the...</span></span></span>

A weather balloon or other high-altitude object is technically possible within the broad CADORS classification. Yet the public record gives no information about a visible shape, drift, altitude or radar track that would specifically support a balloon. Transport Canada notes that large balloons are regulated and may carry transponders or radar-reflective material where required, but no published follow-up has connected the Baffin Island sighting with a known launch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/binder/4-high-altitude-object-incidents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada4. High Altitude Object Incidents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada4. High Altitude Object Incidents</span></span></span>

A conventional aircraft is another general possibility, particularly when viewed almost head-on, but the changing colours do not neatly establish normal navigation lights. Red, green and white are standard aircraft-light colours, yet their apparent pattern depends on viewing angle, and blue is not a standard external navigation-light colour. More importantly, there is no reported traffic comparison, transponder identification or air-traffic-control finding in the public record. The aircraft hypothesis remains possible but unsupported.

Claims involving a secret vehicle or technology beyond known aviation face a greater evidential problem. Nothing published from the incident demonstrates exceptional speed, acceleration, physical proximity or manoeuvring. Such interpretations add assumptions that the original report does not require. The lack of identification leaves a gap; it does not establish that an extraordinary object occupied that gap.

## Why the case remains unresolved

The Baffin Island incident remains unresolved mainly because the evidence was never developed beyond an initial occurrence report and brief press coverage. Transport Canada notes that follow-up to unusual aerial reports can involve Transport Canada itself, NAV CANADA, the Transportation Safety Board or the North American Aerospace Defense Command, depending on the nature of the event. No publicly documented follow-up has emerged showing that radar data, weather information or defence sensors independently confirmed the 2018 light.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/binder/4-high-altitude-object-incidents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada4. High Altitude Object Incidents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada4. High Altitude Object Incidents</span></span></span>

The absence of a published investigation should not be mistaken for evidence that officials concealed a conclusion. CADORS receives thousands of reports each year and is designed to identify safety issues, distribute occurrence information and support broader trend analysis. A light that caused no reported conflict, damage, injury, diversion or aircraft malfunction may not have met the threshold for an extensive public investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tc.canada.ca/en/aviation/publications/aviation-safety-letter/issue-2-2021/civil-aviation-daily-occurrence-reporting-system-cadors" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tc.canada.ca">[Transport Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tc.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Transport Canada The Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transport Canada The Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System (CADORS</span></span></span>

Later reporting has not materially strengthened the original claim. Most subsequent articles repeat the same small body of facts: the date, the Nolinor flight, the changing colours, the CADORS category and the pilot’s own willingness to consider a natural explanation. Some UFO-oriented retellings add dramatic language, but they do not supply new cockpit testimony, sensor records or physical evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufoinsight.com/ufos/cover-ups/the-flight-nrl796-ufo-incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufoinsight.com">[UFO Insight]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufoinsight.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufoinsight.com.</span></span></span>

The case is therefore neither convincingly debunked nor strong evidence of an extraordinary craft. A stellar or atmospheric explanation fits important parts of the description, particularly the colour changes, but cannot be proved without knowing the light’s direction, elevation and duration. Other ordinary possibilities remain open for the same reason. The proper classification is **unidentified because the record is incomplete**.

Within Nunavut’s UFO history, the sighting matters less for what it proves than for what it reveals about evidence from remote northern aviation. The report is credible enough to preserve as a genuine observation and too sparse to resolve. That combination explains both the incident’s enduring interest and the firm limit on what can responsibly be claimed about it.
