## Introduction

[Quebec]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-quebecs-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s 2015 UFO surge was real as a **surge in recorded reports**, but the evidence does not show a matching eruption of extraordinary aerial activity. The Canadian UFO Survey counted 450 Quebec cases that year—about 35 per cent of Canada’s 1,267 reports—compared with 177 Quebec cases in 2014. Quebec’s tally therefore rose by roughly 154 per cent in one year, while the national total increased by about 24 per cent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Quebec_51603e_quebec_2015_reportin_e06c14-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for 2015 Surge" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The most convincing explanation lies in the way sightings were collected. A Quebec civilian organisation supplied an unusually large share of the national database, local newspapers actively solicited and publicised witness stories, and online forms made reporting easier. The resulting numbers measure public participation, organisational reach and media attention as much as anything occurring in the sky. Most Canadian cases remained distant lights or poorly documented observations, while only a small minority combined genuine strangeness with strong supporting information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

## How large was the 2015 increase?

The raw figures were striking. Quebec produced 450 reports in the Canadian UFO Survey, ahead of Ontario’s 314 and British Columbia’s 224. Montreal alone accounted for 97 cases—the highest city total in Canada—while Quebec City contributed another 32. Quebec had ordinarily supplied between about 5 and 15 per cent of Canada’s annual reports; in 2014 its share was already unusually high at roughly 17 per cent, before jumping to about 35 per cent in 2015.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

That made Quebec the principal reason Canada’s national total rose from 1,021 cases in 2014 to 1,267 in 2015. Removing the Quebec increase changes the picture considerably: the rest of Canada recorded broadly similar numbers across the two years rather than anything approaching Quebec’s dramatic rise. The provincial surge was therefore not simply a local reflection of a uniform Canadian “flap”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

Nor was 2015 marked by one dominant Quebec incident comparable with the 1990 [Hotel Bonaventure]({{ 'bonaventure/' | relative_url }}) sighting. Reports were distributed across numerous places and dates, with Montreal and Quebec City leading because they contained large populations and active channels for submitting accounts. Nationally, August was the busiest month, followed by September, but the survey did not identify a single physical event capable of explaining Quebec’s annual total.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

## Why reporting systems drove the numbers

The clearest clue appears in the survey’s own source breakdown. Twenty-eight per cent of **all Canadian reports** in 2015 had been filed with AQU, almost three times its usual contribution. The compilers described this increase as surprising and said its precise cause was unclear. Because Quebec itself represented about 35 per cent of the national total, AQU submissions accounted for a large majority of the province’s apparent excess.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

This was not an official government reporting system. The annual survey was assembled by Ufology Research, a civilian project, from participating investigators, voluntary online databases, websites, private organisations and a small quantity of government information. Nearly 30 per cent of the national cases came through the Mutual UFO Network, 28 per cent through AQU and about 13 per cent through the United States-based National UFO Reporting Center. Less than one per cent originated through Transport Canada or the Department of National Defence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

Consequently, the provincial totals were sensitive to how visible and active local organisations happened to be. A province with a well-publicised French-language reporting service could collect many accounts that might remain unrecorded elsewhere. The following year’s survey made this interpretation more explicit: Quebec again produced an unusually high share—38.5 per cent in 2016—which the compilers attributed to several active civilian UFO groups running public-awareness campaigns and receiving substantial media attention. That persistence supports an organisational explanation better than the idea of a brief, unexplained invasion of Quebec airspace in 2015.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/47454cbc-3003-4ce4-95dc-048cbb6954ae/2016%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2016 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2016 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

A later federal review by the Office of the Chief Science Advisor described Canada’s UAP-reporting landscape as fragmented. It specifically identified several Quebec organisations, including AQU, Réseau OVNI-ALERTE and GARPAN, alongside separate [aviation]({{ 'aviation/' | relative_url }}), police, military and civilian channels. With no single national intake and assessment system, recorded volumes can reflect which organisations are operating, how actively they seek reports and how their material is incorporated into secondary databases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The 2024 Earnscliffe survey for the Sky Canada Project found that 27% of the 1,008 respondents reported having observed an object or phen...</span></span></span>

## Media attention created a feedback loop

[Quebec media]({{ 'media-loop/' | relative_url }}) did more than report the finished 2015 statistics. During the year, the *Journal de Québec* published an interactive map of witness accounts collected by AQU and invited readers to explore stories from communities across the province. It followed with collections of individual testimony and, in early 2016, prominent coverage of the annual figures and AQU’s selection of its strangest cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.journaldequebec.com/2015/05/04/ou-des-ovnis-ont-ete-observes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journaldequebec.com">[journaldequebec.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journaldequebec.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ou des ovnis ont ete observes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grâce à notre carte interactive, consultez les témoignages de citoyens qui ont aperçu des objets volants non identifiés (ovnis) au cours...</span></span></span>

Such coverage can produce a straightforward feedback loop:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A witness sees an ambiguous light but initially does nothing.
* A newspaper article or interactive map shows that other people have reported similar experiences.
* The witness learns where and how to submit an account.
* Additional submissions generate a larger headline, which prompts still more people to report past or recent observations.

</div>

This does not mean witnesses fabricated their experiences. It means the probability that an experience entered a database increased. UFO statistics are counts of reports received, not a direct sensor-based census of unusual objects. The Canadian survey itself proposed easier internet access and portable technology as plausible reasons why more people were recording sightings, while its later Quebec analysis emphasised public-awareness campaigns and publicity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

Media exposure may also influence how an observation is described. A distant aircraft, planet, lantern, meteor or satellite can be genuinely puzzling to someone without enough contextual information. Once UFO stories are prominent, “UFO” becomes an available label for that uncertainty. The label records the witness’s inability to identify something at the time; it does not establish that the object lacked an ordinary explanation.

## Why reports are not necessarily separate events

A further complication is the difference between a **report** and a distinct aerial event. Several people may witness the same light and submit separate accounts. One person may report to more than one organisation. A story may be reposted on a website and later gathered into a national compilation. Conversely, several lights observed together might be treated as one case.

The 2015 survey drew material from numerous organisations and internet sources, but its published methodology does not provide a detailed, auditable account of how every possible cross-database duplicate was detected and removed. It should therefore be read as a carefully assembled catalogue rather than a precise count of 1,267 independently verified physical events. The report itself acknowledged that compiling the database required extensive searching of online sources and that much posted material received little or no follow-up investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

This distinction matters particularly in a year when one reporting organisation suddenly supplied almost three times its normal share. A rise in collected submissions might represent more witnesses, more retrospective reports, better outreach, greater overlap between channels or some combination of these. The available publication does not break down the Quebec increase finely enough to assign exact proportions to each mechanism.

## What were people actually seeing?

The national dataset does not resemble a collection dominated by close, structured craft. In 2015, 641 cases were classified as nocturnal lights and 387 as nocturnal objects that appeared to have a definite shape. Fewer than two per cent belonged to close-encounter categories. The most frequently recorded shape was a point source—essentially a star-like or distant light—with 572 examples. Disc or “flying saucer” descriptions numbered 45, less than four per cent of all reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

Duration also offers clues. The average report lasted about 16 minutes. The survey noted that very short observations often prove compatible with meteors or fireballs, whereas sightings lasting an hour or more are frequently astronomical objects whose apparent motion results from the Earth’s rotation. Duration alone cannot solve a case, but a prolonged stationary light generally calls for checking planets and bright stars before considering more unusual explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

The average reliability rating fell significantly in 2015. According to the survey, more cases contained minimal witness information, incomplete descriptions and little or no investigation. Many originated as website submissions, second-hand postings or self-published accounts. The compilers warned that well-investigated cases made up only a small fraction of the available material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

Those limitations weaken any attempt to turn the Quebec total into a claim about extraordinary aircraft. A large sample of brief narratives can be useful for studying reporting patterns, recurring misidentifications and public perceptions, but it cannot substitute for calibrated [photographs]({{ 'photographs/' | relative_url }}), radar data, precise bearings, weather records, aircraft checks or independent measurements.

## What does “11.5 per cent unexplained” mean?

Across Canada, 146 of the 1,267 cases—11.5 per cent—were placed in the survey’s “unexplained” or “unknown” category. That figure is sometimes treated as though investigators had eliminated every ordinary possibility and confirmed something exotic. The survey explicitly rejected that interpretation. “Unknown” meant only that no satisfactory conventional explanation could be proposed from the information available at the time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

The classification was also subjective. Cases were assessed by contributing investigators and the survey’s compilers, without a single rigorous standard applied by a government scientific agency. The report recognised that investigators’ prior attitudes could affect decisions: a believer might classify a case as mysterious more readily, while a strong sceptic might favour a conventional interpretation. Cases could also be reclassified if better information emerged later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

More importantly, only 65 cases—about five per cent of the Canadian total—were both “unknown” and rated at least six out of nine for strangeness and reliability. That higher-quality subset was national, not a Quebec-only figure. The published statistics do not demonstrate that Quebec’s unusually large report count produced a correspondingly unusual concentration of strong cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

The remaining national reports were divided among explained cases, probable or possible explanations, and insufficient evidence. In 2015, 66.3 per cent received a probable explanation and 20.5 per cent lacked enough evidence for a confident judgement. The “unexplained” percentage therefore describes the limits of the collected information, not the percentage judged extraterrestrial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

## What the surge tells us about Quebec

Quebec’s 2015 numbers are historically interesting because they show how dramatically a civilian reporting network can reshape a national UFO map. The province moved from 86 reports in 2013 to 177 in 2014 and 450 in 2015, then retained an unusually large national share in 2016. That pattern tracks the expanding visibility of Quebec’s reporting organisations and media coverage more clearly than it tracks a single coherent series of aerial incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c23c8b29-268f-4742-a45e-2dba156b0e52/downloads/ad7fee54-0561-4e08-b287-dcc28c3bd87c/2015%20Canadian%20UFO%20Survey.pdf?ver=1774235759532" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: img1.wsimg.com">[img1.wsimg.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">img1.wsimg.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2015 Canadian UFO Survey</span></span></span>

The surge should therefore be described neither as meaningless nor as proof of extraordinary craft. It documents hundreds of people who encountered something they could not immediately identify and chose to report it. It also exposes the weaknesses of Canada’s decentralised system: uneven public awareness, overlapping databases, inconsistent investigation and very limited official input.

Within Quebec’s wider UFO history, 2015 matters chiefly as a lesson in interpreting statistics. A famous incident such as Hotel Bonaventure can be assessed as one event with a defined place, time and witness group. An annual surge is different. Its meaning depends on who collected the reports, how widely reporting was promoted, whether accounts described independent events and how much evidence accompanied each submission. On those measures, Quebec’s extraordinary 2015 number is best understood as a reporting and publicity phenomenon containing many ordinary ambiguities—and a much smaller residue of cases that remained unresolved.
