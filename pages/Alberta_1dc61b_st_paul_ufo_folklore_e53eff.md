## Introduction

St Paul became [Alberta]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-albertas-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s “UFO town” by building a monument, not by experiencing a famous alien encounter. Its UFO Landing Pad opened on 3 June 1967 as part of Canada’s centennial celebrations, combining small-town promotion, Space Age optimism and an unusually expansive message of welcome. The attraction was formally opened by Paul Hellyer, then Canada’s minister of national defence, and is recognised as the world’s first official UFO landing pad.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stpaul.ca/visitors/ufo-landing-pad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stpaul.ca">[stpaul.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stpaul.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo landing pad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. PaulThe Landing PadIn 1967, a proposal was made to build the world&#x27;s first UFO Landing Pad as a landmark for the Town. That distinction is important. The landing pad is genuine civic history, but it is not physical evidence that an unidentified craft visited St Paul. Its lasting significance lies in how successfully the town turned flying-saucer imagery into local identity. Over nearly six decades, the concrete platform, tourist centre, conferences, souvenirs and community events have kept UFO folklore visible even as particular sightings elsewhere in Alberta have remained disputed, unexplained or forgotten.

## The landing pad as a civic landmark

The landing pad emerged from the enthusiasm surrounding Canada’s hundredth anniversary of Confederation. St Paul pursued numerous centennial projects, and its local museum records the mood in the words of mayor Jules Van Brabant: “Centennial fever has this town on fire.” Among the projects, the UFO pad was the one that escaped the anniversary year and became a permanent national curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stpaulmuseum.ca/centennial-projects/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stpaulmuseum.ca">[St. Paul Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stpaulmuseum.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. Paul MuseumCentennial ProjectsAs stated by then Mayor Jules Van Brabant, “Centennial fever has this town on fire.” Notable projects w...</span></span></span>

The idea was deliberately imaginative. Rather than commissioning another conventional statue or memorial, local promoters created a futuristic landmark suited to the culture of 1967. Human space flight was prominent in public life, the first crewed Moon landing was still two years away, and “flying saucers” were already familiar through newspapers, films and popular discussion. St Paul’s project therefore worked on two levels: it was playful enough to attract attention but serious enough in construction and ceremony to become more than a temporary joke.

The official opening strengthened that effect. Paul Hellyer arrived while serving as minister of national defence, giving the ceremony a connection to the federal government and Canada’s military establishment. That did not make the site part of an official UFO investigation or defence programme. His role was ceremonial, and later stories about Hellyer’s personal beliefs concerning extraterrestrial life should not be projected backwards onto the town’s original purpose. In 1967, the pad was principally a centennial landmark and statement of hospitality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/first-official-ufo-landing-pad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: guinnessworldrecords.com">[Guinness World Records]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">guinnessworldrecords.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guinness World RecordsFirst official UFO landing pad &#124; Guinness World RecordsThe world&#x27;s first official UFO landing pad was built in the...</span></span></span>

Its design made the message tangible. The raised concrete structure carries a map of Canada, while the wording beside it declares the ground beneath the pad “international” territory and expresses the hope that outer space will remain free of national conflict. Visitors “from earth or otherwise” are welcomed. The language is fanciful, but its underlying subject is recognisably terrestrial: peace, safe travel and international goodwill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/first-official-ufo-landing-pad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: guinnessworldrecords.com">[Guinness World Records]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">guinnessworldrecords.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guinness World RecordsFirst official UFO landing pad &#124; Guinness World RecordsThe world&#x27;s first official UFO landing pad was built in the...</span></span></span>

This is why describing the attraction merely as a tourist trap misses part of its historical character. It belongs to a wider generation of Canadian centennial projects that used optimistic architecture, civic participation and national symbolism to imagine the country’s future. The UFO imagery gave St Paul an attention-grabbing form, but the commemorative message was unity rather than a claim that extraterrestrials had already arrived.

## How a centennial novelty became local identity

Many novelty monuments receive brief publicity and then fade. St Paul’s landing pad survived because the town continued to use it. Municipal tourism material still presents it as the community’s best-known attraction, and the visitor information centre beside it extends the site beyond the original concrete structure. The centre has also been promoted as a UFO Data Centre, although its public role is primarily tourism, interpretation and local visitor services rather than scientific investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stpaul.ca/visitors/ufo-landing-pad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stpaul.ca">[St. Paul]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stpaul.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo landing pad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. PaulThe Landing PadIn 1967, a proposal was made to build the world&#x27;s first UFO Landing Pad as a landmark for the Town. The adjoining tourist centre, officially opened in the 1990s, marked an important change. The landing pad was no longer simply a preserved relic from 1967; it became the anchor for an ongoing visitor experience. Displays, UFO-related material, souvenirs and locally made goods allowed the story to be retold to successive generations. The Town of St Paul and provincial tourism organisations now describe the attraction in terms of openness, welcome and community character as much as extraterrestrial mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stpaul.ca/visitors/ufo-landing-pad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stpaul.ca">[St. Paul]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stpaul.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo landing pad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. PaulThe Landing PadIn 1967, a proposal was made to build the world&#x27;s first UFO Landing Pad as a landmark for the Town. Public events have periodically refreshed the folklore. A UFO conference held in St Paul in July 1998 reportedly attracted almost 500 registered attendees, including visitors from several Canadian provinces and the United States. The programme belonged to civilian ufology rather than official research, but the turnout showed that the landing pad could act as a meeting place for believers, sceptics, investigators and the merely curious.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uforum.blogspot.com/2016/02/the-st-paul-ufo-landing-pad.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uforum.blogspot.com">[uforum.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uforum.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the st paul ufo landing pad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the st paul ufo landing pad</span></span></span>

The same process continues. In July 2026, St Paul staged its inaugural UFO Days, a three-day community event built around the town’s landing-pad identity. Its emphasis on family activities, entertainment and local participation illustrates how the symbol now functions: not as an emergency facility awaiting alien contact, but as a flexible theme for festivals, tourism and civic storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cfweradio.ca/2026/07/07/st-paul-to-host-inaugural-ufo-days-event/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cfweradio.ca">[CFWE Northern Alberta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cfweradio.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CFWE Northern Alberta St.Paul to Host Inaugural UFO Days Event</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CFWE Northern Alberta St.Paul to Host Inaugural UFO Days Event</span></span></span>

This longevity has given the site a place within Alberta’s wider roadside-attraction culture. The province is known for large, unusual or humorous landmarks that make small communities memorable to motorists. St Paul’s distinction is that its object is not simply oversized. The landing pad creates a miniature story world: the visitor is invited to imagine who might arrive, why the town has prepared for them and what sort of welcome they would receive.

That story travels well in photographs, television features and short news pieces. The Provincial Archives of Alberta preserves images of the 1967 opening, while later coverage by Canadian and international media repeatedly returns to the contrast between an ordinary Alberta town and its extraordinary invitation to interplanetary travellers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flickr.com/photos/alberta_archives/53444848946" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flickr.com">[Flickr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flickr.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">alberta archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Official Opening of UFO Landing Pad, St. Paul, Alberta, 1967Albums (1) · Canada · Alberta · UFOs · aliens · UAPs...</span></span></span>

## Tourism folklore and public memory

St Paul’s UFO reputation is folklore in the broad sense: a shared story repeatedly told, adapted and performed by a community. It does not depend upon everyone believing that spacecraft will use the pad. The humour works precisely because residents and visitors can participate without agreeing on whether extraterrestrial visitation is possible.

Several recurring ideas sustain that folklore.

**The town is prepared.** The landing pad turns a hypothetical event into a piece of municipal infrastructure. Its concrete weight, official ceremony and public plaque give an absurd proposition the appearance of practical planning.

**Everyone is welcome.** The original text expands prairie hospitality to “all intergalactic beings”. That phrase allows the attraction to represent friendliness rather than fear. Unlike UFO stories centred on pursuit, secrecy or abduction, St Paul’s narrative begins with an invitation.

**Government took it seriously.** Photographs of a federal defence minister at the opening can easily be detached from their centennial context. In folklore, ceremonial participation becomes part of the joke: Canada did not merely tolerate the project; a senior minister helped inaugurate it. The factual core is real, but it should not be mistaken for federal validation of extraterrestrial claims.

**Perhaps something will eventually land.** Tourism descriptions often remark that the pad has not yet received an alien visitor “to their knowledge”. This knowingly unprovable qualification keeps the joke alive without making a testable factual claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.travelalberta.com/listings/ufo-landing-pad-20618" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: travelalberta.com">[Travel Alberta]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">travelalberta.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on travelalberta.com.</span></span></span>

The monument also benefits from the way memory condenses complicated history. A visitor may not remember the exact date, the centennial programme or the names of local organisers, but “the Alberta town with a UFO landing pad” is easy to retain. A 2018 Historica Canada and Ipsos survey cited in a scholarly history of Canadian UFO investigation found that 27 per cent of respondents correctly identified St Paul’s pad as the world’s first. That result suggests the attraction has entered national popular knowledge well beyond the town itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digitalcollections.trentu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/2022-04/A_History_of_Canada_s_UFO_Investigation_1950_1995.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digitalcollections.trentu.ca">[Digital Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digitalcollections.trentu.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A History of Canada s UFO Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A History of Canada s UFO Investigation</span></span></span>

Its success has nevertheless created opportunities for exaggeration. Online retellings sometimes blur together the landing pad, alleged local sightings, Paul Hellyer’s later public statements and broader claims about government UFO secrecy. Those elements belong to different periods and have different evidential status. The securely documented history is straightforward: St Paul built a centennial monument, a defence minister opened it, and the town subsequently developed tourism and public events around the theme.

## How local culture differs from case evidence

The most important distinction is between a **cultural artefact** and a **UFO case**. A case begins with an alleged observation or physical event: witnesses report an object, investigators collect testimony, records are compared and possible [explanations]({{ 'explanations/' | relative_url }}) are assessed. St Paul’s landing pad began with a municipal decision to construct a landmark. It has no originating sighting that requires explanation.

That makes the pad very different from Alberta incidents such as the 1967 ground marks near [Duhamel]({{ 'duhamel/' | relative_url }}). At Duhamel, investigators considered impressions in a field, witness accounts and possible ordinary causes. The debate concerns what produced physical traces and whether the available evidence is adequate. At St Paul, the concrete itself is fully explained; the questions concern symbolism, tourism and public memory.

The distinction can be expressed plainly:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **The landing pad is well documented.** Its construction, opening date and civic purpose are supported by municipal, archival and contemporary records.
* **It confirms public interest in UFOs, not alien visitation.** The monument demonstrates that flying-saucer culture was strong enough to shape a Canadian centennial project.
* **Its government connection was ceremonial.** Paul Hellyer’s presence did not convert the site into a Department of National Defence installation or investigation centre.
* **Its UFO Data Centre is interpretive and touristic.** The name evokes research, but the facility operates primarily as a visitor centre and community attraction.
* **Later conferences and festivals show cultural persistence.** Attendance at such events measures interest, identity and tourism value, not the truth of particular extraterrestrial claims.

</div>

This difference matters when assessing Alberta’s UFO history. Folklore can preserve memories of real reports, but it can also merge unrelated stories and reward the most entertaining version. A monument may become better known than a carefully investigated case because it is visible, photographable and easy to visit. Popularity is therefore not a measure of evidential strength.

St Paul also offers a useful counterpoint to the darker side of UFO culture. Many twentieth-century narratives revolved around military secrecy, invasion or personal danger. The landing pad instead presents outer space through optimism and hospitality. Its plaque imagines safe travel and freedom from conflict. Even when later UFO culture became more conspiratorial, St Paul’s central public message remained welcoming rather than fearful.

## Why St Paul still matters in Alberta’s UFO history

St Paul matters because it shows that provincial UFO history is not composed solely of unexplained lights and disputed testimony. It also includes the ways communities use those stories. The landing pad transformed a passing cultural enthusiasm into a durable place, one that residents could promote, visitors could photograph and journalists could revisit whenever public interest in UFOs rose again.

Its survival also reveals why folklore often outlasts evidence. Individual sightings are usually brief and poorly recorded. A concrete monument can be maintained, repainted, archived, celebrated and incorporated into annual events. St Paul gave Alberta’s flying-saucer culture a permanent address.

The resulting identity is neither wholly earnest nor wholly satirical. Local promotion accepts the joke, but the town has invested real effort in preserving the site and explaining its history. UFO enthusiasts can treat it as a landmark of Canadian saucer culture; families can enjoy it as roadside novelty; historians can read it as a product of centennial optimism; and sceptics can appreciate it without accepting any extraordinary claim.

That range of meanings is the key to its endurance. St Paul did not become Alberta’s UFO town because it proved that visitors came from another world. It became Alberta’s UFO town because, in 1967, it publicly declared that they would be welcome—and continued telling that story long after the centennial celebrations ended.
